# Chapter 6: The Captain's Ledger

The net had gone down into the sealed levels before first light and come back with nothing: no warm signature, no breach at depth, no citizen accounted for. That was the finding, in the grid's own hand: *nil*. Vance read the word twice. In a city that never stopped recording, nil was a sentence, and never just a word. The sweep report had run sixty-one lines; the first reading was for the numbers, the second for the edges. The close-out in the flat had closed itself the same: *no record*.

One. The net found nothing.
Two. The net had been told to find nothing.
Three. Nothing has edges. This nil had none.

He called in the sergeant of the detail. Torelli: clean book, twelve years, a man whose reports had always read like a measured floor. This statement had three sentences, and the middle one had what the other two did not: an edge.

'There was a wall where the map keeps a passage, sir. I checked the map twice. That's all.'

'A wall.'

'Concrete. Sealed. New.' Torelli offered the words like keys. 'The map says passage. The wall says no. I stood in front of it, and the instruments agreed with the wall.'

'For how long?'

'I don't — '

The sergeant's face did not change. It went smooth, like a sentence losing its referent. Vance watched the man's mouth close on nothing, the way he had watched Renn's mouth, Dalby's mouth. He wrote the three sentences down before the amendment could take them. Line by line. On paper.

He pulled the strings. The city kept a record of every mind wearing its product; the product had recorded Torelli in that minute: the readout came up even, finished, dustless. The wall. The sealed concrete. The passage never open in any record. Vance set the readout against the page he had just written. The readout was the city's memory and the page was his. They had not agreed. The edges were all on the page.

One. The grid amends.
Two. Minds take the cut.
Three. Paper holds.

He went back to the beginning of the file and read it twice: Renn and Dalby, the closed close-out, the flat that was still being watched by men who reported, once a day, that nothing was there.

He had pulled their strings that morning as a matter of habit. The strings had been amended overnight, as the close-out had amended itself: the drawn weapons now smooth minutes, the apologies now nothing. The flat itself: a wall. What the strings still carried, in the city's own hand, was the shape of a man: Renn forty, Dalby young, their histories intact, the gaps exactly where the anomaly had stood.

The city had taken the two minutes out of their account and left the men themselves standing. The minutes were the whole of the arithmetic; the men were just the carriers. He sat with the two pages open and thought about the word *amended*, which the city used as a clerk uses *reconciled*, and he wrote:

> 3-0713. Time 05:40. The net found nothing. The net was told to find nothing.
> Torelli: a wall where the map keeps a passage. The wall held instruments.
> The statement slipped while I watched. The strings support the wall.
> The book has the wall. The wall is not in the grid.
> Decide what it means that the men who were there do not know they were there.

Then he went down.

The ramp was older than the boundary. The gate was keyed to a dead grammar he had stopped needing to read years ago. The cold came up out of the dark in stages, colder than twenty-one, colder than anything he had stood in for a decade. The air at the bottom was the weight of a room that had been closed a long time and was still trying to be a room. It smelled of rain, or what rain left behind when it had been taken out of the sky. Torelli came with him, at his shoulder. Vance took no lights. 'Cold,' Torelli said.

'Yes.'

'It gets colder the further down. Every time.'

He said it as other men state the weather, and Vance, who would write it down, nodded. The instruments would see as far as they were allowed to see.

The level gates were concrete, wet with the cold, each carrying its number pressed into the face at the pour: two, four, six, eight. Vance read them as he passed and kept the count for the page he would write. The grid's copy of the same numbers had gone smooth. The last gate took his retainer, cycled, and closed behind them with a dull clank of steel on steel, deadened by the wet. Its inner face carried condensation in small even beads, shed onto concrete no hand had touched since the formwork came off. Nothing else had come this way. The water said so, and water could not be amended.

The dark was the levels' own medium. He had come down to stand in it. He counted every step, a habit older than the book, older than the drawer. The instruments read clean. Then cleaner.

They had gone a hundred metres down the old street before he felt it.

It came on him like pressure comes on a man before the wave. The same thing had happened at 01:48: a room's temperature shifting, unsourced. The anomaly's attention, arriving like the grid's attention but older, and pointed. He stood still in the dark and let it arrive. It came up against the inside of his skull like water against a sealed hatch. It did not enter.

One. The press is real.
Two. It cannot pass the grid in me.
Three. It is standing farther down the street, and it knows I am the first mind it has not taken.

Torelli went smooth beside him. He heard it happen: the breathing changing, the feet going still, a man standing where a man had stood, with nothing inside the standing. Vance counted. Torelli came back after seven seconds, with no memory of the seven seconds. Vance wrote it down before asking anything else.

At the edge of the instruments' reach, in the old light, something stood. A shape. It would not resolve. It had the height of a small thing, and it held the darkness as the flat's sill had held a shape for two armed men who could not say what it had been. Vance looked at it across a hundred metres of dust. It looked back as one looks at a locked door. Then it moved off down the street, deliberately, unhurried, as if the meeting had already told it everything it needed to know.

It made no sound at all. Everything Vance knew about the anomaly said it never did.

'What — what was that, sir?' Torelli said. His voice was the measured floor again, but the floor had taken a minute out of him. Vance had that minute in his book, inked and dated.

'It was the thing the flat's report said wasn't there.'

They walked the level until the light gave out entirely and the instruments began to shiver in the smoothness the visit had left behind. Then they turned back. Vance walked back silent. Torelli kept pace beside him; both were used to the quiet the city kept. He was writing in his head, line by line, as he had been taught to write when the grid was watching and the pen was the only unamendable thing in the building.

In the office, with the door closed, he wrote:

> 3-0714. Time 06:55. Descended. The anomaly came to me and could not enter.
> Torelli said it gets colder the further down. He was right.
> The grid in me is military grade. It held. It is the one product the city makes that the city's own product cannot pass. I have never been more certain of the city's arithmetic, and never less certain of what it is for.
> Torelli lost seven seconds. He does not know it. The strings will not show it. This page will.
> It stood at the edge of the instruments' reach and looked, and left. It spent nothing on me. It knows what I am, or it does not care, or it is saving what it has. All three keep the same ledger: it will not waste itself on the one mind it cannot touch.
> The men are already saying it. *The anomaly will not enter the captain's mind.* They say it the way men say a wall will hold. I record it and let it stand.

Torelli brought the coffee in the tin cup he always brought. The building had a brewer that made six identical cups; the sergeant had noticed, years ago, that Vance drank the one that was not the machine's. He set the cup on the exact corner of the desk, where Vance could reach it without looking, and stood back. He stood against the door, looked at the open book, and asked, once, the question the building had been asking for years, with the same patient curiosity:

'Why the book, sir? The grid keeps everything. It's faster, it's complete, it never forgets.'

'It doesn't keep,' Vance said. 'It smooths. A wall where the map says a passage, and the grid believes the wall. There has to be one medium in the city that remembers what the grid smooths, or order is just the grid agreeing with itself.'

'And it has to be paper?'

'Paper holds. It can't be edited by anything but a hand, and a hand leaves pressure in the fiber. The pressure is a witness.'

Torelli looked at the book a moment longer, low, as you look at a thing you have decided not to ask about again, and said: 'With respect, sir — you could — you could let the records hold it. You don't have to carry it in your head.'

Vance picked up the pen. He said it to Torelli; as the pen came down on the page, he said it, in the same breath, to himself.

'Order is not because I don't remember. It's because I do.'

The pen made the line in the book. Torelli put a hand on the doorframe and went out. Vance turned the pages back: the morning's pages, the day before, the day before that, back through the book to its first page. Then, past the book, to the shelf behind his desk, where the first volume stood with its spine gone soft.

The first volume stayed closed. He put his hand on it as he had put his hand on it two nights ago, and held it, and took it back, and sat down at the terminal.

The complete file came up the way files came up: a chute, a life, an order. Cover: ARTHUR M. Retainer, marked. IRREPARABLE — the stamp the city used for the work it did not intend to finish, and had never amended in twenty years.

The old leaves: the household flag, 2087; the raid authorization, in the city's older hand; the confiscation record — one picture book, graded high, grade his.

The boy's voice behind the door. The record held nothing of it. No record had ever contained it.

And, dated the month of the raid, a single leaf, the oldest in the file:

NEURAL LOCK. SUBJECT: ARTHUR M., MINOR, AGE 8. GROUNDS: MNEMONIC RETENTION, IRREPARABLE. SCOPE: PERMANENT. IRREVERSIBLE. — and the delegation line, and the signature.

He had read the order before. He had filed it, at twenty, which was to say he had put it out of reach. The signature was his.

It had been his before he turned the leaf. He had known it as a man knows the number that is going to come up; the knowing was only the shape the memory had left behind it. For twenty years he had remembered the raid and not remembered the signature. Both had been true. The page was the place the two truths met.

He looked at his own name on the order. The hand was the hand he had had at twenty; the V open like a door; the letters overran the line like a man bowed under a weight he had agreed to bear. The same hand had opened this file. The same hand wrote the book in the lower drawer. The same hand had gone down into the sealed levels this morning and come back with the anomaly's press recorded in it, line by line.

One. I led the raid.
Two. I signed the lock.
Three. The boy has carried it for twenty years.
Four. The anomaly is with him now.
Five. It cannot enter me.

The city had armored the hand it trusted with a pen. The wall that held against the anomaly in his skull was the same engineering — no. He stopped the thought before it finished. He was the boy's opposite, and it would be too easy to write himself the answer: that the boy had always been the thing the lock was for. He was not going to write that sentence in any book. A book that kept it would be a book that lied.

He closed the file and sat. The flat's watchers had reported, once a day, nothing; the boy was below, in the sealed levels, with the anomaly. The anomaly could not enter Vance, and it had not needed to enter the boy, and the two facts stood in the same column. The grid believed in the wall Torelli had met. It was smoothing the boy out of its own memory, the way it had smoothed the weapons and the apologies out of Renn's strings and Dalby's. If Vance waited on the grid, in three days there would be no boy — only a wall, a stamp that said IRREPARABLE, and a first volume on a shelf.

The anomaly was outside the minds' order. It lived in the levels' order. It amended records and minds. There was a class of tool that never touched records or minds. It cared nothing for what a document remembered. It burned the coupling between a thing and its place in the world, and left the amendable world intact around it.

The stores existed for the city's own keeping, the flushing of its dead rooms. The array had been drawn on a hunt exactly once in his tenure, and never on the sealed levels.

He typed the request himself. It was short. It did not mention the boy or the signature on the order. The form wanted a ground, and the drop-down ended at *civil disturbance*, and the field was marked *required*, and he typed the truth into it, and the form accepted it, being required to accept. He had never had cause to thank the form before. The request line had no category for either:

> Requisition: EMP stores, tactical field array, one unit, drawn on Captain Vance, Enforcement. Grounds: sealed-level anomaly, class unamendable, immune to standard interdiction. Addressee: Command, Requisitions.

He signed it. The pen carried his name as it had carried it for twenty years; the V opened as it had opened at twenty, in the month of the raid, at the bottom of the order. Two signatures, twenty years apart, and the same door.

He sat with the request open and waited. The evening did its work on the city; the page did not change. The answer came back in the grid's even hand at 23:52. He read it twice.

*GRANTED.*

He logged the draw with the level gates. He ordered the array staged at the bottom of the ramp by first light, so that the tool would be waiting there, in the cold, before the city had finished amending the night. Then he turned to a clean leaf and wrote:

> 3-0715. Time 23:52. EMP stores drawn. Approved.
> The wall in Torelli's report, the nil in the sweep, the boy under the level the grid is smoothing out of its own memory — a tool exists that does not care what a mind remembers.
> The one mind it cannot enter has asked for the one tool it cannot evade.
> Watch the levels.
