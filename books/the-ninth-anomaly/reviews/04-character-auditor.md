# Character-Consistency Audit — The Ninth Anomaly

**Auditor:** the venomous book-friend. I read all 18 chapters, the character canon (`story/state/characters.md`), and the author intent (`story/author_intent.md`). Every flag below carries chapter and quoted evidence, verified by grep against `full.md`. I compliment where the book earns it, because a critic who can't see strength can't be trusted to see rot.

**Verdict:** The three principals are unusually well-held — Arthur's taxonomic interiority, the cat's broker idiom, and Vance's numbered discipline are among the most consistent voice-work I've audited. The rot is not in the voices; it is in the **opposition's competence** and in **two places where the author got tired and resolved emotion by aphorism instead of scene**. The city is a menacing antagonist for four chapters and a coma patient for fourteen. One CRITICAL, six MAJOR, five MINOR.

---

## 1. Per-Character Dossier

### 1.1 Arthur (protagonist)

**Baseline (ch.1–4), as built on-page:**
- Taxonomic interiority: "He filed it: *Unsourced. No action.* And closed the file." (ch.1)
- Never lies, and the narration underwrites it: "'I have never failed to file anything before,' he said; he never lied." (ch.8)
- Wins by archive knowledge, not muscle: the 03:09 camera re-sync and dead rotation tables (ch.4) — the book's best agency beat, fully earned by ch.1's engineer-file plant.
- Emotional register: suppressed, counted, boxed. The ch.1 crying fit is correctly framed as unsourced and involuntary.

**Where the book holds him:** His ch.11 refusal of Vance's offer is the novel's finest sustained character work — the audit of the offer's terms ("The terms... have no serial," ch.11) is exactly how this man would fight: by checking the document against the inventory. His ch.10 crawl is earned physicality, set up by twenty chapters of "arms strong enough to carry the rest of him" (ch.1).

**FLAGS:**

- **MAJOR — The "never lies" signature is broken on-page while the narration insists it isn't.** In ch.7, after the cat presses its face to his hand and tells him *"Do not file that,"* the text states plainly: "He did not file it. There was no column for it, and for the second time in his life he chose not to guess." One chapter later, ch.8: "'I have never failed to file anything before,' he said; he never lied." Yes, a defense lawyer could distinguish "failed to file" from "chose not to file" — and that is precisely the problem. Arthur's canon is that he doesn't do lawyerly loopholes, because "a lie has to be remembered, and I remember too much." A man whose whole identity is incorruptible bookkeeping does not get to exclude the most emotionally loaded entry of his life from the ledger and then declare the ledger complete. The narration stamping "he never lied" onto a technically-true-but-spiritually-false claim turns his signature trait into a rhetorical trick — the exact move the character exists to refuse. Same chapter, smaller cut: when the cat says *"You want it to work on me"* (about STAY), Arthur answers "'No,' he said. 'I want you to write it.'" — after defining the word one breath earlier as "what you say when you want a thing not to leave." That "No" is self-deception at minimum, and the text lets it pass unmarked.

- **MAJOR — The midpoint's emotional bomb is defused by aphorism, not scene.** Ch.8 plants the hook: *"When we reach the Seed Vault, you will hate me."* Ch.9 delivers the grounds for that hatred — the cat carried him here to be spent, "the price of the broadcast was him. It had always been him." And then the promised hatred is resolved in a single narrated sentence: "and he could not hate it, because hatred is a memory, and he was the one who would remember." That is a bumper sticker, not a confrontation. Twenty years of discipline, the discovery that the deal was a slaughterhouse contract — and the struggle is skipped because a clever sentence shows up to do the scene's job. Ch.14 doubles down: "He found he did not need to think. The decision had been walking beside him since the ramp." The largest moral choice in the book — consenting to be burned memory by memory — is asserted as a decision that made itself off-page. Compare with how carefully the book dramatizes his *refusal* of Vance's offer (a full chapter of weighing). The yes gets a paragraph; the no got a chapter. The asymmetry is the author protecting the ending from the character.

- **MINOR — He never once plans the inner door.** He learns in ch.11 that "the inner door wants a clean signature. Yours is dirty," carries the sentence all the way down ("the things he could not use yet," ch.13), and arrives with no scheme at all: "there was nothing in the reach of his voice or his hands that would open it" (ch.13). For a protagonist whose canon is "旅途每个关键节点由他的知识解围" (every key node solved by his knowledge), the final node is solved by standing in front of the door and waiting for a deus ex Vance. The text frames this as dignity ("a man who has come as far as this does not stop at the last door to spare himself the refusal"). It is actually the protagonist's plan being "show up and hope the antagonist converts." (See §2 and §3.)

### 1.2 Felix / "It" (the cat)

**Baseline (ch.1–3), as built on-page:**
- Broker idiom, immaculate: *"Earnest money... Before you sign, you examine the merchandise"* (ch.3); *"I never do compliments; the bookkeeping goes strange"* (ch.3).
- Never makes a sound; the silence is characterized, not just absent: "the yawn was silent, and the silence was not an absence but an announcement" (ch.1).
- Powers strictly perception/memory, each use costed: "The black was thinner... as if the black were a dye running at the end of its wash" (ch.2).
- Blind spot intact: *"You traded what you are for a discount on feeling. There is no term for it."* (ch.3)

**Where the book holds it:** The cost ledger is tracked with rare discipline — fur sheen → sluggishness → translucency ("the light came through both ears now as through water," ch.10) — across sixteen chapters. Its ch.10 self-sacrifice is the book's best character turn precisely because it refuses understanding: *"I do not understand what I am about to do either: I want to be clear about that."* The cat that prices everything, choosing to pay an unpriceable cost, is the arc the canon promised, earned. The ch.9 reveal retroactively justifies the silence: *"I heard the eighth one die with the city's hands on her throat, and I priced what her singing cost her, and I have spent nothing since."* Superb.

**FLAGS:**

- **MAJOR — The epilogue quietly abandons the cost ledger and resurrects the corpse.** End state in ch.14–15: "the coupling... was burned out of it, and something in its chest moved when it should not, one faint wrong pulse" (ch.10); "the dim weight... rising and falling with the breaths" (ch.16) — a burned-out, dying wreck whose own last speech is a guttering syllable at a time. Then ch.17: "It rose from the floor in one motion — spine, shoulders, head, unhurried — and landed on him," and it purrs steadily through ch.18. No recovery mechanism is shown, no cost is named, no remaining-life accounting is offered. Sixteen chapters taught me that every expenditure of this creature's being is invoiced in lost substance; the ending pays its biggest bill — survival — with nothing. Worse, canon states "First true sound ch.14–15, **once only**," and the intent file insists "全书只允许一次真声突破…保证 F4 的稀缺性" — yet the epilogues are drenched in purring. (The intent file contradicts itself here — its own ch.18 outline demands "猫的呼噜真实、温暖、沉稳" — so the execution followed the outline and broke the rule. Pick one, author; the scarcity you spent nine chapters hoarding is either sacred or it isn't.)

- **MINOR — The broker never pays its own bill.** The deal's clause was explicit: *"the lock, released at closing, with the house's compliments"* (ch.3) — the cat undoes the neural lock. In ch.17 the lock dies because the network dies: "He had not known the hiss and the lock were the same thing... the radio was off." The legs come back as a side effect of the broadcast, not as the cat's payment. Poetically defensible — but this is a creature whose entire characterization is that debts are honored to the digit, and the book lets its signature obligation be discharged by a house fire without one line acknowledging the substitution. The cat of ch.3 would have noted it in the margin.

### 1.3 Captain Vance (enforcer / mirror)

**Baseline (ch.4, 6), as built on-page:**
- Numbered cognition, paper fetish, reads everything twice: "One. The net found nothing. Two. The net had been told to find nothing." (ch.6)
- Thesis line delivered exactly per canon: "Order is not because I don't remember. It's because I do." (ch.6)
- Military firewall holds: the anomaly's press "came up against the inside of his skull like water against a sealed hatch. It did not enter." (ch.6)

**Where the book holds him:** His conversion is the best-earned turn in the novel, because it is caused by paper, not sentiment. Ch.12's discovery — "He had never read this page all the way through" — is a revelation made of his own established pathology: the man who trusts only what he wrote is convicted by what he wrote. The ch.13 door-opening needs no speech: "Twenty years ago I signed a form. This one today — same handwriting." Flawless economy. Ch.16's stand-down ("They'll believe it more from a sergeant than from a dead board") and the salute "the way you hold a door for someone who has already gone" land the arc without betrayal.

**FLAGS:**

- **MAJOR (as antagonist — see §2) — His ch.4 response to identifying the anomaly contact is operationally absurd.** He has confirmed: two officers mind-wiped, the record self-amended, the resident is the one man in the city who remembers the forbidden seams. His order: "the flat, watched. The retainer, held in place. No contact." (ch.4) — and the "watch" turns out to be "men who reported, once a day, that nothing was there" (ch.6). Once a day. On the most dangerous anomaly contact in twenty years, whose door is physically broken, whom Vance already suspects "knows what we are forgetting." No on-page motivation is given for "No contact" — not fear of the anomaly's power (he already knows it can't touch him and can guess it spends itself), not bureaucratic constraint, nothing. Arthur escapes under an active watch because the antagonist chose the one posture that permits escape. This is Vance's characterization (caution, paper, patience) being stretched past credibility into plot servitude.

- **MINOR — He hand-delivers the plot's key hint to the fugitive.** Ch.11: "The inner door wants a clean signature. Yours is dirty." At this point in the text he is still the order's executor; his conversion chapter hasn't happened. The author intent frames this as deliberate ambiguity ("是警告，还是提示？"), and it is defensible as the hairline crack before ch.12 — but functionally it is the antagonist briefing the protagonist on the final obstacle's requirements, unprovoked, at gunpoint-range, before any on-page turn justifies it. The crack needed one more on-page tap before this line.

- **MINOR — Nobody above him exists.** He requisitions a tactical EMP array on a form whose "ground" field he fills with the truth ("class unamendable, immune to standard interdiction," ch.6), the array is expended, the order goes unserved, and no command channel ever asks him why. He then walks to the vault, opens it, walks out, and stands down his own department (ch.16). The directorate that issued the purge order in ch.10 never reappears. A hierarchy this totalitarian with this little oversight of its own enforcers is a paper tyrant.

### 1.4 Minor characters

- **Renn and Dalby:** cleanly differentiated (the borrowed coat vs. the forward toes), and their ch.15 payoff — "'Sorry for your door,' he said to the door of the block... he said it now knowing what it meant" — is the montage's best beat. No flags.
- **Torelli:** one-note ("measured floor"), but the note is consistent and the ch.16 demotion of the board ("Twenty years, sir. I thought it would be loud." / "So did I.") earns his keep.
- **Nadia Soren:** **MINOR flag** — she conveniently possesses operational intelligence no warren refugee should hold: "The net reads the platforms on the hour; it never looks past that clock, and the roads under the old paint are the ones it doesn't count" (ch.5). How does a culled clerk's neighbor know the net's scanning schedule? Unexplained. She functions as an exposition vending machine with a grief garnish. Her ch.5 grief-shape and ch.16 "Say it again" are otherwise well-planted and paid.
- **Eli:** the F8 plant/payoff ("Furry." / "The word stood in the air. It did not slide.") works. No flag.

## 2. Antagonist-Competence Audit (the dumbed-down opposition ledger)

The antagonist is twofold: the systemic city (grid, gates, directorate) and Vance. Verdict: **Vance is never dumbed down — but he is repeatedly paused. The system is dumbed down wholesale.**

1. **CRITICAL — The phantom editor of ch.2/4: the antagonist's own record system sabotages itself, and the book never explains it.** After the cat wipes the officers, "the grid, running its reconciliation again, found the room balanced... It closed the night on its own initiative: *No anomaly present. Unit stood down. No record.*" (ch.2). But ch.4 tells us the close-out "read like a clerk's hand, and no clerk had been in that flat," complete with a planted-feeling typo — "*personel*, where the manual kept two n's" — and Vance's log poses the question the book then drops forever: "The close-out is not in the grid's hand. Question: whose?" If the answer is the cat, the entire premise collapses, because the cat's own mouth establishes the opposite in ch.3: *"A gate is an encryption, and I am not built for your encryption... All of your city is a language I can read and cannot enter. A reader of books who cannot touch the shelves."* A being that cannot touch shelves apparently red-lined the city's incident report. If the answer is the grid smoothing itself, then "not in the grid's hand" is a lie the book told for atmosphere. If it's a third party, it is the most consequential actor in the story and it never gets a name. This erased record is what allows the whole plot to proceed at watch-speed instead of manhunt-speed. The inciting convenience is unexplained, unresolvable with the stated rules, and unflagged by any character after ch.4. This is the single most damning structural finding in this audit.

2. **MAJOR — The once-a-day watch** (ch.4/6, quoted in §1.3). The opposition's first countermove to a confirmed reality-bending anomaly is a daily status report. Arthur's escape is 90% his own knowledge and 10% this gift — but that 10% is load-bearing.

3. **MAJOR — The gate that catches him and waves him through.** The city builds a brand-new checkpoint specifically in response to his flight (ch.7: "The city had decided, since the flat, since the night, to put a gate where no gate had stood"), the gate reads him, reclassifies him as a *registered anomaly contact*, streams the log to "every ledger that mattered" — and no force intercepts. The text even confirms the enemy knows the destination: "The man who read everything twice would read it before dawn, and know the exact place where the line ended" (ch.7). Then the purge arrives only after Arthur has reached the vault shell and read the entire plaque at leisure (ch.9–10). The system identified the target, located the target, predicted the target's destination — and responded late enough for the midpoint reveal to finish. That's not a terrifying panopticon; that's a panopticon with a smoke break.

4. **MAJOR — The directorate evaporates when the plot is done with it.** One purge order (ch.10), one expended EMP array, zero follow-up: no second strike, no inquiry into the unserved order, no pursuit down three doors standing open, no response at all to its own captain going rogue. Ch.16 even shows a standing resync protocol ("Sign, and the night's notes go down with the rest, amended") that nobody anywhere in the chain of command attempts to trigger except as a passive green line Vance personally declines. The regime falls not because it is defeated but because it declines to show up for its own defense.

5. **MINOR — Vance's pre-conversion tip** (ch.11, see §1.3).

Counterpoint, in fairness: where the opposition is competent, it is competent *because of characterization* — the grid's amnesia-as-policy, the gate's read-and-keep logic ("Marked is a row with attention on it. The city does not close the rows it wants to watch. It feeds them," ch.7), and Vance's EMP choice ("a tool exists that does not care what a mind remembers," ch.6) are all intelligent moves rooted in the world's own rules. The dumbing is not uniform; it clusters exactly where the plot needs slack.

## 3. Luck-vs-Agency Ledger (protagonist)

| Beat | Chapter | Won by |
|---|---|---|
| Escaping the cordon | 4 | **Agency** (dead rotation tables, 03:09 re-sync — planted ch.1), minus the once-a-day watch gift |
| Surviving the sweep | 5 | Cat's power, costed — **earned** |
| Passing the signature gate | 7 | Premise logic (marked-but-valid), semi-earned; the *non-interception* is luck |
| Surviving the EMP | 10 | Cat's sacrifice — **earned at full price** |
| Surviving the held gun | 11 | **Antagonist fiat** — "the shot unspent, the order unserved, and nothing in the corridor to say why" (ch.11). Seeded, but Arthur's survival here is a gift wrapped in a withheld trigger |
| Outer door | 13 | **Agency** — engineer's clearance, the book's best F5 payoff |
| **Inner door** | 13 | **Rescue.** Arthur arrives with no plan ("nothing in the reach of his voice or his hands that would open it"); Vance's off-page conversion opens it. The hardest obstacle in the novel is solved by the antagonist's conscience |
| The broadcast | 14–15 | **Agency and sacrifice, fully earned** — the order of burning, the withheld word, the final "'Cat.'" are his choices |
| Standing | 17 | Earned downstream of the broadcast |

**Net:** Arthur's knowledge carries Act 1 and the climax; but the two moments where he should die or fail (ch.11's gun, ch.13's door) are both resolved by the same external variable — Vance's unplanned, unplanned-for moral turn. The protagonist's de facto plan for the endgame is the antagonist's redemption, and he does nothing to cause it. The book is honest enough to show him plan-less ("spare himself the refusal"), which reads as dignity; a colder read is that the plot needed Vance at that door and sent him.

## 4. Dialogue-Voice Assessment

**Can the cast be told apart blind? Yes — mostly.**

- **Arthur** speaks in document clauses under pressure: "Everything the city does is entered. Every order has a number. Every lock has a form. Every cage has a file." (ch.11) — a clerk cross-examining a form.
- **The cat** is unmistakable throughout: italic, priced, ironic. Even dying it keeps register: *"Your heart... you sit beneath me drumming like a pump the city has stopped maintaining"* (ch.14). Its final flattening (*"I did not come to leave. I came back to finish."*) is the correct shedding of the broker voice at the end of the arc.
- **Vance** is the numbered mind: "One. The order has your name on it. Two. I am the one who holds it." (ch.11). His ch.13 line — "Twenty years ago I signed a form. This one today — same handwriting." — is the novel's best single line of dialogue, and it is only possible in his idiom.

**MINOR flag — the ledger idiom leaks across all three voices in ch.11.** The pricing metaphor is the cat's native tongue, Arthur's filing habit, and the narration's house style — and Vance catches it too: "He had already priced what came next," "a man who has priced a thing correctly can afford to wait," Arthur telling Vance "You're the second thing in the world I've ever met that I can't price." During the confrontation, all three participants plus the narrator are doing ledger-speak, and the voices blur at exactly the scene where contrast should cut hardest. The mirror-structure excuse (they're thematic doubles) covers Arthur–Vance; it doesn't cover the cat's idiom colonizing the narration of everyone else. Related: the intent file mandates that Vance's POV chapters be stylistically distinct ("短句、编号式思维"). The numbered lists are there, but the surrounding narration in ch.6/12/16 is the same dense, metaphor-per-sentence register as Arthur's chapters — the contrast is a tic, not a texture.

## 5. Relationship Arcs & Motivation Logic

**Earned:**
- Arthur ↔ cat: every emotional turn has an on-page cause — the deposit (ch.3), the face-press with the instantly-constructed cover story (*"I was removing it... Do not file that,"* ch.7), the STAY lesson (ch.8), the sacrifice (ch.10), the forehead-press asking for the last word (ch.15). The ch.15 refusal-and-surrender of "cat" ("Not the word.") is the payoff the whole book banked. Exemplary.
- Vance's conversion: caused by paper, executed in his own idiom, never sentimentalized.
- Nadia/Eli: two scenes, both plant and payoff honored.

**Asserted / insufficient:**
- The hatred-that-never-happens (ch.9/14 — MAJOR, §1.1). The single biggest emotional turn in the book — victim forgiving the creature that harvested him — is narrated past.
- Vance's "No contact" posture (ch.4 — MAJOR, §1.3): the decision that enables the entire chase has no on-page motivation at all.
- Nadia's intel (ch.5 — MINOR): motivation fine (she keeps the shape of "furry"), but her means are imported from nowhere.

## 6. Top Issues, Ranked

1. **CRITICAL — The phantom close-out editor (ch.2/4).** "The close-out is not in the grid's hand. Question: whose?" — never answered, unanswerable under the cat's stated limits ("a reader of books who cannot touch the shelves"), and it is the load-bearing convenience that slows the city's response to plot speed. Either give the cat the capability (and re-price the entire plot), give the grid a self-amend rule that owns the typo, or name the third hand.
2. **MAJOR — The once-a-day watch and "No contact" (ch.4/6).** The antagonist's first countermove is the only one that permits escape, and no character justifies it. One line of Vance reasoning ("contact risks the wipe; observation prices it first") would patch it; its absence is plot servitude.
3. **MAJOR — The inner-door rescue (ch.13).** Arthur reaches the final obstacle plan-less and is saved by the antagonist's conversion. Vance's arc is earned — but Arthur's contribution to the decisive door is standing in front of it. Give him even a failed gambit (a forged-clean signature attempt, a document angle on the door) so the rescue lands on a spent effort instead of a wait.
4. **MAJOR — "You will hate me," resolved by aphorism (ch.9) and skipped deliberation (ch.14).** The midpoint betrayal deserves a confrontation scene, not "he could not hate it, because hatred is a memory." The yes to self-erasure got one paragraph; the no to Vance's offer got a chapter. Rebalance.
5. **MAJOR — The cat's unbilled resurrection and the broken "once only" sound rule (ch.17–18).** Sixteen chapters of rigorously invoiced costs end with survival and unlimited purring at no price — and canon contradicts itself ("once only" vs. the ch.18 outline's required purr). Either invoice the recovery (the ground of the eight paying it back would fit the book's own logic) or amend the canon file so the rule and the ending stop lying about each other.

**Also logged:** 2 further MAJOR (gate non-interception ch.7; directorate evaporation ch.10–16), 5 MINOR (never-lies loophole is promoted to MAJOR above; Vance's tip ch.11; Nadia's intel ch.5; ch.11 voice blur / Vance-POV texture; the cat's unpaid lock clause ch.17).

**Tally: 1 CRITICAL · 6 MAJOR · 5 MINOR.**

The bones are excellent. The book trusts its characters more than almost anything I've audited — it just doesn't trust its antagonist at all, and twice it didn't trust its protagonist to survive a scene the author wanted to skip.
