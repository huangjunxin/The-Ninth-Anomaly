# The Ninth Anomaly

# Chapter 1: The Thing on the Windowsill

Arthur woke at 06:12, the exact minute he always woke, the minute before the static rose.

Something in the room was warm, and it should not have been.

The room was twenty-one degrees. It had been twenty-one degrees every day of his life: the register under the window sealed, the glass tinted and fixed, the air through the vent a manufactured product, like his food, like the silence of the sky. He lay still and read the room by feel, the way he read everything — chair, desk, the fold of the blanket. One item answered wrong.

The sill was warm.

Warmth implies a source; a source implies permission — and nothing in the flat had permission to be warm. He ruled out the possibilities one by one: the register, sealed; his own body, cold below the waist, a machine in standby; his hands, which had not touched the sill since cleaning day. He was left with the one category the city had no name for, because the city had no name for anything it had not made.

He got up. The getting up was a strict procedure, and he did not trust his thoughts inside it, so he did not think: hands flat, hips lifted, the dead halves swung clear while his arms took the census of him. Brake. Lever. Settle. He rolled on his palms, one quiet arm-stroke at a time, not waking the chair, and crossed to the window. He touched the sill with his knuckles.

Not furnace-warm. Not flesh-warm. A slow, settled warmth, as if something the size of a small animal had been sitting there a long time and had left. He held his knuckles against the glass for a full minute. The warmth began to leave while he held them, reluctantly, the way a rumor leaves a room when someone actually looks for it. By the time he was dressed, it was gone.

He filed it: *Unsourced. No action.* And closed the file.

The hiss in his skull was the implant — thirty centimeters of locked code and a dead uplink, twenty years old, officially described as *disconnected*. Not broken. The city never admitted broken. Just no longer connected to anything that mattered, a radio left between stations. He dressed, made his morning inventory — the day, the year, who he was; the box at the back of it stayed shut — and ate.

The pack was a beige rectangle of calibrated texture and uniform temperature. It had no smell. Smell was a layer the city had removed from food the way it had removed birds from the sky.

He flexed his gratitude, because gratitude was work and he did it daily: at the pack, at the vent, at the doors that opened for his badge, at the ramps that remembered his wheels. The city was good to him. It had to be. It had taken so much of him that anything it gave back looked like a gift.

He had a room, a job, a body that worked from the waist up — and arms strong enough to carry the rest of him. The only legs he was ever going to be introduced to.

At 07:40 the door opened for his badge, and he rolled out into the corridors of the Archive. The lights came up at his approach and dimmed behind him.

The Forbidden Archive was a sub-basement, and the whole of its architecture was in that: the city built downward to store what it could not burn. Failures of the screening did not die. They were shelved — a second death, cleaner than the first.

The floor was a long room of carrels, six rows running east to west, each carrel a screen and a man or a woman in it, every one of them marked, locked, and aware of it in a way that never needed to be spoken. The severance had not taken on them. The city, unable to make them forget, had made them useful instead: those who can see are used to bury what they see.

Arthur hung his coat on the hook behind his seat, pulled the gloves from the drawer, and settled the chair into the exact angle the floor had worn for him. Then he logged in. The day's work rose onto his screen from the chute below — confiscated text, one item at a time, sorted, entered, sentenced. Most of it was harmless. The screening was a blunt instrument, and the archive was where its mistakes accumulated: a tract on the migratory patterns of a bird that no longer existed, an illustrated farming manual, a child's primer that had tripped the scanner on a word the city had decided weighed too much.

Two carrels down, the woman he did not speak to went blank.

He noticed, because he always noticed. Her eyes went soft. Her hands paused one beat. And the beat was gone, absorbed into a blankness that left nothing behind. The tract went back into the chute. She moved on, her face smooth as a sealed register, and said, to her screen, "Must be the weather." Arthur said nothing. Nobody looked up. Nobody on the floor ever asked anybody what they had read today.

He knew what the system had done, though he did not know how he knew: it had not deleted the content — deletion was too honest. It had cut the wire between the thing and the feeling, leaving the concept pinned like a dead specimen, with no memory of the pinning. For him the cut had never quite closed. That was why he was down here, on the payroll of his own least useful organ: a memory that would not stop.

The primer reached his screen at 11:03 — a children's book, confiscated, the family gone. Somewhere in its pages the scanner had found a picture and a word it had flagged *excessive*. The flag sat in the margin like a small red burn.

He turned the pages with a gloved finger. The pages were thin and soft with age; the word surfaced — the old word, the one the city had chased for twenty years and never quite killed — and he did not let it land.

It was the first word he had learned to hide. This was the discipline, built slowly at eight, built so well that he was now very good at his job: to touch forbidden things and feel nothing. Nobody had ever been better equipped for it than a child taught to hide the thing he felt most.

He had hidden it from the doctors, from the technicians, from the polite men in the corridor who asked what he remembered, and at last from himself, as much as he could. What he could not hide was the rest: hiding required remembering, and remembering was the one thing the lock had never reached. It had reached something else instead.

He had been eight; he had woken one afternoon and his legs were gone and the lock was in his head, and he had never been told which was the price of which.

Twenty years he had assumed one transaction — the lock for the legs, or the legs for the lock. His own file had crossed his carrel once, in a batch of his records, stamped with the word the doctors had settled on when he was eight: *IRREPARABLE*. He had read it and felt nothing, because he agreed with it. The legs had been the price of keeping the word, and he had kept the word, and the account had always felt exactly balanced.

The engineer's file came up at 14:47 — an old infrastructure archive, condemned on general principles, the kind of thing the city kept because it was thorough and would not burn. Arthur read the index page, and then he read it again. The coordinates were not in the city's format.

Current files used sector, block, floor — four digits and a hyphen, the grammar of a city built in a hurry and regulated since. This one used three fields, axis, ring, depth — *AX-7 / R-3 / D-24* — a layout he had not seen on anything dated after the Culling, signed with the crest of a department the city no longer ran, descending into a structure below the grid the current maps did not keep.

To anyone else it was a dead file. To the system it was nothing, which was why it had surfaced here, where the city put things it had decided were not worth remembering. Formats were the first things he memorized: they carried nothing, so the city never banned them, and they survived. He collected them the way a museum collects the last of a thing.

He memorized the engineer's format deliberately, which was different from how he memorized everything else, and did not ask himself why — asking would have meant opening a file he had agreed never to open. *Axis, ring, depth.* The format settled in him the way the warmth had sat on the sill that morning.

He was still carrying it when he came home.

The route was fixed: out of the sub-basement, across the plaza where the sky was a color the city had decided on. The plaza held no birds; he had stopped looking up years ago, but the absence had a weight of its own, and tonight he noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth. On a bench, a man was stroking the air before him slowly, the way you stroke a pet. Arthur looked away. Looking was a habit he had trained himself out of.

The evening was blue and even. The flat door opened for his badge. The sill was a sill.

The thing on the windowsill was not.

For a long moment he did not move. It was black — black the way a drain is black, a black that did not reflect the room so much as end it — and it sat with its tail laid around its feet, looking at him. He stopped the chair in the middle of the floor. The door shut behind him. The room, steady at its usual twenty-one degrees, seemed to have gone one degree quieter.

He looked at it, and kept looking. Looking was what his species did wrong: it looked, and the thing lost its weight and became a specimen. Nothing slid off this one.

The thing on the sill met his look and held it, and he understood, with a cold, clear certainty, that he was being looked at — not a look he had manufactured, but one that had been waiting a very long time to be received. People had glanced at it before. Their glances had gone through and come out the other side, like light through a broken pane. No one had ever actually seen it. No one had ever been an audience. And he, Arthur — marked, locked, irreparable — was the first.

It understood before he did. He saw the understanding arrive in it, a small adjustment along its spine, a recomputation. It did not flinch; that would have conceded something. With perfect, unhurried insolence it raised one hind leg and began to wash its face, as if his arrival were the interruption.

It made no sound.

That was the first fact he took careful note of, because the room had gone quiet the way a room goes quiet when it is holding something. It yawned — a slow, deliberate hinge of a mouth — and the yawn was silent, and the silence was not an absence but an announcement.

Then it folded its eyes shut, not a blink but a slow fold, like a ledger closing, and he understood that it was smiling: not with its mouth, but with the whole arrangement of itself. Its tail wrote a long, lazy verdict in the air and laid it down. Every motion was a statement in a language that had no sound in it — none of it for him, all of it for him.

The arrangement of it was familiar. It took him a moment — the word was a box he did not open — and then the memory came up through the floorboards of him, unstoppable, because it had been waiting all day behind the warmth and the format and the empty sky.

A page of a book. A picture, and in the picture a cat, a striped tabby, asleep on a windowsill with the sun laid over it like a paid debt, and under the picture a caption his mother had read aloud, once, in a voice he had filed under the heading he never opened: *A cat keeps the sun the way a child keeps a secret.*

He was looking at the thing the picture had been of. The category came to him then, all at once, and with it the word, and he did not let it land — twenty years of discipline, and the word reached his lips and was met at the border and turned back. The thing on the sill watched him do it, watched the word come and go, and its folded eyes held a judgment he had no name for.

The afternoon came up behind the word, and he could not stop it: thirty-one degrees, the kind of heat the city no longer permitted, heat that came through an open window without permission, and his mother's voice — the same voice that had read the caption — saying the last ordinary thing she ever said to him before the polite men came in: *when I count to ten, you'll be somewhere else.* He had never learned where the ten was supposed to take him. The door had opened first.

He did not open the box. Opening was for the Archive. The flat came back to its twenty-one degrees, the light outside went through its quiet evening changes, and the thing on the sill stayed where it was — the first thing in twenty years that had ever looked at him without a label or a verdict.

It stood up.

The stand was a performance — a long, ceremonial stretch that began at the spine and ended at the tips of the toes, a stretch that said, in the soundless language, *I was going to do this anyway, and you will not remember it correctly.* Then it came down off the sill, across the desk, across the arm of the chair, and landed on his knees.

He felt nothing.

The legs were dead. Twenty years dead; they did not report; they had not reported since the day they were exchanged, in the one transaction, for the word. He felt nothing where it landed, and this was true, and he clung to it like a man told not to look down. But the chair leaned one degree into the weight. His hips, still his, read the shift and passed it up his spine. And on his knees, inside the dead zone, there was a weight — a small, settled, impossible weight, warm the way the sill had been warm that morning, warm the way the afternoon had been warm, twenty years ago, thirty-one degrees, warm the way he had not allowed himself to be warm since.

He could not feel it. It was on him anyway.

The tears arrived before he understood them. They arrived the way the warmth had arrived, unsourced, and he sat in the chair with the black thing on his dead knees and cried, and he did not know why, and the thing on his knees looked at him with its unspent eyes and did not look away, because it was, for the first time in its long and patient existence, being watched.

# Chapter 2: Knock

The city balanced its books at night. Arthur was awake when the building ran its reconciliation, and so he was the only one who saw it happen: at 01:00 exactly the sensors in the flat re-read the room, as they had every night of every year of his life, and this time they found a surplus. Heat without an owner. Mass without a citizen number. The register under the window was sealed and the vent delivered a manufactured product, and the room had been balanced — it was always balanced, twenty-one degrees, one occupant, one chair — and now it was not. The grid did not know what it had found, because the grid did not know that kinds of things existed; it knew only that the ledger would not close.

It flagged the surplus, graded it, and routed it, forty-two seconds from fault to dispatch, and below him, not looking up, the building began to move: the elevator called down to the service floor, a door answering a door, the corridor cameras shifting their angles one by one, the way a room shifts when someone has decided to enter it.

He had lived twenty-eight years under that attention and understood its grammar the way he understood every grammar: first the questions, then the knock, then the door.

He laid out his options in their proper order, the way he laid out everything. The door had one lock and the badge in his name. The window was fixed glass in a fixed frame above a fixed drop. The balcony had been sealed before he was born. The legs, which might have carried him anywhere, were dead, and would not be consulted. *No action.* He sat with the chair's brake on, and the black thing on the sill made the same silence it had made all evening.

Twenty years ago the men had not knocked. They had entered, the morning the book was taken, and that had been that. The city had grown more courteous since, or more confident: it knocked now, a warning, a formality, before it came through.

The footsteps reached the corridor at 01:47, unhurried — the unhurried stride of people who had all night and the right to use it. Two sets of them, and a third presence below the floor of hearing, a vehicle waiting in the service lane, receiving. Then the knock came at 01:48: three knocks, spaced, exact, the way the city did everything it considered a courtesy. He counted to ten, the old way, the way his mother had taught him — *when I count to ten, you'll be somewhere else* — and, as always, the door opened first. It did not open for a badge. It opened for a single practiced blow beside the lock, delivered with an economy Arthur had only ever read about, the lock coming out with the frame, the door swinging in on one sound.

Two men came through the frame in the correct order. The first — Renn, though nobody would have said so — was perhaps forty, and carried the job the way an older man carries a borrowed coat: comfortably, and without much respect. He moved like a man who had done this exact thing a hundred times and had stopped being interested in it. The second — Dalby, young enough to stand with his weight forward on his toes — came one step off Renn's shoulder, his earpiece loud enough that Arthur could hear the voice in it, a voice that belonged to a third man who was not in the room. 'Vance,' Dalby said, half turned away. 'We're in.'

They did not look at Arthur. He was already a known quantity; the corridor had briefed them: *twenty-eight, retainer, archive, locked, irreparable, no threat.* They read the room instead, with their eyes and with the instruments on their wrists, and the instruments did the work their eyes would not, because the room had a surplus in it and both men's eyes slid off that surplus and could not land. They trusted the instruments. 'Window,' Renn said. 'Sill.' And the instruments painted a shape, and the shape was where the cat sat: black like a drain, tail laid around its feet, watching the two men the way it had watched Arthur the evening before, with the unhurried insolence of a thing being interrupted in its own room.

The weapons came up. Not quickly — correctly, Dalby's first, Renn's a half-second behind, a formality of their own, and Arthur felt the air begin to hold its breath, and he could not move, and the black thing did not move, and that was the first wrong thing, that a thing watched by two weapons should have done something. It did nothing. Its nothing was absolute, a statement, the same statement it had been making since it landed on his dead knees and looked at him: *I was going to do this anyway, and you will not remember it correctly.*

'Subject,' Vance's voice said, resolving out of the earpiece's carrier.

'Unidentified,' Dalby said. 'It's — '

The sentence died. It had been dying in that room since the evening; in the presence of the thing that refused to be completed, the mind arrived at the shape and slid off it, and the mouth was left holding a silence.

'Human, animal, or object,' Vance said.

The two men said nothing. The instruments said: *mass. heat. undetermined.*

'Repeat the string,' Vance said. Renn repeated it. 'Slower,' Vance said, and Renn, who had known the captain for years, repeated it slower, and did not say what he thought, which was that Vance always wanted things twice, as if he did not trust the words to stay where they were put.

'Animal?' Vance said, and the old word came over the air, a word of the old world, and Dalby, holding the shape in the corner of his eye, found that he could not attach it. The shape was not in any class the two of them held. It was a category error standing on a windowsill. 'It's not animal,' Dalby said. 'It's something.'

'Something,' Renn said, agreeing with the only word that would fit.

Arthur sat in the chair with his hands on the armrests and the box shut under the discipline. He did not say *don't*; he said nothing; he had been trained, at eight, by people who asked what he remembered, to keep what he saw where no one could see it, and whatever he was looking at now was inside the room. The guns had not lowered.

The cat yawned.

A slow, deliberate hinge of a mouth, with two weapons aimed at the center of it, and the yawn made no sound — the silence of a thing that had never once spent any of itself on noise.

Then it looked at Dalby.

His own implant's hiss never changed pitch, the entire time. It had nothing to report; whatever was passing through this room went through the dead instrument without registering, into the one mind in the building it could not touch, and the hiss went on exactly as it had gone on for twenty years, a radio left between stations.

The change did not arrive as an event. It arrived as a subtraction, and Arthur, who had spent twenty years learning to read subtraction, watched it move through the younger man and meet nothing that refused it.

Dalby's trigger finger straightened. His elbows loosened. His shoulders came down by fractions, one service of a machine losing pressure, and his face — the professional face, the face of a man whose job was to be unreadable — softened into something unguarded, and then into something puzzled, and the muzzle of his weapon came down, gently, the way a man sets down something breakable.

Renn held two seconds longer. His hands were older and his discipline had had more years to set, and Arthur watched him resist — not with the weapon, with the habit — and then the habit let go too, and the second muzzle came down, and both men stood in the broken doorway with their arms empty and the room's quiet inexplicably total.

'Dalby,' Renn said.

'What?'

'Why are we — '

'Why are we what?'

Renn looked at his own hand, at the holster his hand had gone to, and the puzzle arrived on his face. 'I don't know,' he said. 'I thought you — '

'I never said anything.'

'I'm sorry,' Dalby said. 'I thought you said — ' And then, with a sincerity that stopped Arthur's breath: 'I'm sorry. I don't know why my hand went up.'

'I'm sorry too,' Renn said. For a long moment the two of them stood in a stranger's flat in the middle of the night, perfectly embarrassed — two grown men who had drawn weapons upon nothing and could not say which of them had started it — and the apology went back and forth between them like a coin, each man pressing it on the other, each man refusing it, with the formal, baffled politeness of people who had lost their place and were being courteous about it.

'Dalby. Renn. Status.' The voice came out of the earpieces, plain, the voice of a man who kept his words in order and expected others to do the same. Dalby answered the air. 'We're done, sir.' He did not know what he had been doing; the sentence had no referent; it came out of his training, the way the holstering had. 'Report at the vehicle.' 'Yes, sir.' The earpiece hummed. Vance said something else, and then the two men were moving out through the frame — Renn paused at the door, and did a strange, precise thing: he reached back and wedged the door's panel against the frame, the way a man closes a door he did not break, and he said, to the door, or to the room, or, at last, to Arthur: 'Sorry for your door.'

Then the corridor, the footsteps, the elevator answering, and Vance's voice still carrying in the air of the earpieces, growing thinner — 'Repeat. Repeat — ' — the doors closing on it like water closing over a coin.

The building marked their egress. The cameras tracked them to the elevator; the elevator weighed them; the service door let them out into the lane where the vehicle waited, receiving. And the grid, running its reconciliation again, found the room balanced: 21.0, one occupant, one chair. It closed the night on its own initiative: *No anomaly present. Unit stood down. No record.* Everything about the night was gone except the parts the grid could not erase: the splintered frame, the dust, and Arthur, who had watched it all, and knew it.

He filed them as the instruments had — Renn, Dalby — under a heading the city had no label for: *Intervention. Non-physical. Vector: perception. No residue.* Then he took stock of himself, the way the Archive took stock of a chute of returns: the three knocks, the angle of the muzzles, the second the confusion had arrived, the apology and its refusals. All present. All his. Whatever the thing had taken from the two men, it had taken nothing from him, and for a moment he sat with that fact, which had the weight of a door closing. He did not open the box. The box was shut; the box had always been shut; the discipline held, and would hold.

Then he turned to the desk, and the black thing was no longer on the sill. It had come down to the middle of the desk, and the walk had been a transaction, not a ceremony — the tail was not laid, the spine had lost its performance — and it stood, once, in the middle of the desk, and then it assembled itself into sleep: not a curl, not the careful fold it used for rest, but a sprawl, flat out, legs at wrong angles, head on forepaws that were not tucked, the posture of something that had been unplugged in the middle of a thought. And the black was thinner. The evening before, the black had been the black of a drain, a black that ended the room rather than reflected it; now it was matte, dusty, as if the black were a dye running at the end of its wash. The room had gone back to its twenty-one degrees. The surplus was gone — the warmth that had flagged the grid, the warmth that had spent the night — gone the way the warmth had left the sill that morning: reluctantly, and completely.

He wheeled close and looked at it. It slept the way things sleep when they have paid for something. The breathing was shallow and even; no twitch ran down its spine; it did not dream, or it dreamed all the way down, and neither was the sleep of a body. It slept wrong, and it slept long, and Arthur, who had not slept at all, sat and kept watch, because someone had to, and the city had already folded its night away, and only Arthur was awake to remember it.

The rules stood as the night had presented them. The thing worked on what was between a person's ears. It had not repaired the door. It had not moved the dust; the frame was still splintered where the lock had come out; the only revisions were in the two men, where the revisions could not be seen. And the revision had cost. It had bought the night with itself. The black was thinner than it had been at evening, and he understood that there was a ledger under that matte black, and it did not balance.

The morning arrived at its usual hour. At 06:12 — the minute he always woke, though he had not slept; he had sat with the chair's brake on, the breakfast pack unopened on the counter, the room going through its quiet changes — the cat woke. The waking was work. It did not rise the way it had risen that first evening, in a long ceremonial stretch; it reassembled itself in stages, the spine, then the shoulders, then the head coming up last, as if something in it had to be wound before it could arrive at being. It shook itself once, a dry, silent shake — no sound, nothing spent on noise — and then it turned and looked at him.

The look. Yesterday evening the look had arrived: it had come across the room and he had received it, the way you receive a light. This look did not arrive. It went. It came past his eyes, a hand going under a surface, into the dark behind them, and he felt it turn over the first page of him. There were no words in it. The room was silent, the same silence as ever — but he felt it begin to open him, room by room, the way he opened a chute of returns, taking down what was kept there, reading the labels he had spent twenty years not reading. He felt it pause. Somewhere in him, near the place he did not go, near the box, the door that had no handle on the inside — he felt it pause, and consider, and he knew, with the cold unsourced clarity with which he had known everything that night, that it had found it.

It was reading him. Not the way anyone reads — the way he read the Archive, the way the city read a reconciliation: from the inside, where the words were kept. And it had not asked.

The knowing landed, complete, all at once, the way knowing lands when it is finished being avoided. This was not an animal. No animal keeps a ledger. It was a thing wearing the shape of a thing, an instrument that counted, a presence that read — and it was reading him, from the inside, where the words were kept.

It was not an animal.

It was looking into him.

# Chapter 3: The Terms

The look did not end. The attention from the night before had not withdrawn when the light came up; it had simply gone on, a presence still in the room the way a verdict is still in a courtroom after the gavel. Arthur sat with the chair's brake on.

The black thing on the desk reassembled itself in stages — the spine, then the shoulders, then the head coming up last — and then it sat, matte, dusty, the black of a dye at the end of its wash. He understood that he was being observed with an interest no one in the city had ever shown him — flattering, if it had not been so clearly professional.

He had eaten the pack at the proper hour — the beige rectangle of calibrated texture, uniform temperature, no smell — and the thing had watched him eat, unhurried. The hiss in his skull had gone on for twenty years, a radio left between stations. In the small hours he had rehearsed a sentence — *the flat is mine; you are a surplus; please* — and it had never assembled, because none of it was true except the arrangement's presence. So he had said nothing, and at 09:14 the words arrived.

The door was still broken. No one had come to fix it; the ledger had closed on the night, and a door it had not logged was not a door it knew about. The splintered frame stood wedged against itself, a courtesy with no repair behind it.

They did not arrive as sound. He filed that distinction first, with the discipline: *Contact. First. Vector: perception. No sound produced.* The hiss did not change pitch; nothing crossed the air of the room; the words were simply there — already written, already his — and by the time he understood that he was reading them, they had been standing in the room for a full second, patient as a balance, waiting to be noticed.

*"You were about to say 'don't.'"*

He held very still.

*"Don't look. Don't remember. Don't open. You've built a whole grammar out of that word, and you keep it better than you keep anything else. I read it last night. It's the one well-kept room in you."*

'I wasn't going to say anything.'

*"No. You were going to think it. You think in boxes, Arthur; the thought was already stamped and waiting on the chute. I could show you the stamp."*

'You read me,' he said. His voice came out careful and even, a clerk reciting from a form, but his hands were white on the armrests. 'Last night. You went through everything.'

*"You left the door open."* The tail laid one long, lazy verdict along the desk edge and let it lie there. *"Your kind keeps everything within reach, and most of you the cut took: the wire came out, and whatever was left slid off the shelf by itself. But you healed wrong. The cut closed over the wire instead of over the gap, and you kept the connection. That's why you can see me. That's why you're down in that sub-basement. That's why your file says what it says."*

He did not ask. He had seen the word himself. *IRREPARABLE.* The word arrived at the border of his attention and was turned back, and he understood, with a cold clarity, that it had read the word too, in him, and was choosing — for the moment — not to use it. Brokers kept their leverage in reserve.

'What are you?' he said.

*"I'm the thing in the surplus column of your building's books."* It said it as if it were quoting a price. *"It's as much of an answer as you've paid for."*

'What do you want?'

The tail stopped, and the thing on the desk was suddenly, professionally still, and it looked at him with its unspent eyes. He felt the inventory begin again — the presence passing through him, taking the measure of the hiss, the box, the twenty years of weight — and then it told him.

*"There is a place under your city. Below the grid, below everything the current maps admit exists. The Seed Vault. It is the one door in this world that will not open for me, and it is the door that has been waiting twenty years for you."*

He set the name down as it arrived — *Seed Vault* — and it stuck, because it was a noun without a category, and nouns without categories were the only things that survived down in the sub-basement. And underneath the noun something moved: a format, three fields, axis, ring, depth, descending into a structure below the grid the current maps did not keep. He had carried the address for a day without knowing it was an address.

The cat watched him carry it. *"Yes. That's the front door. You've been holding it since before I arrived — the city put it in your hands itself, and you didn't know. I'd wait before thinking too long about that. There are observations in this world that don't bear looking at all at once."*

'What's in it?' he said.

*"Owed things. And the things I came to put back."*

'That isn't an answer.'

*"It's the answer I'm selling. The rest is at the door."*

He sat with the brake on and turned it over the way he turned over a chute of contested returns. 'You want me to take you there.'

*"Carry me,"* the cat corrected. *"There's a difference. And in exchange — "* It looked down at the dead length of him in the chair, the blanket, the footplate, the twenty years, and let the look do the arithmetic. *"— I undo your lock. Not the file. Not the stamp. The lock itself. What was taken from you below the waist, I give back. Head for the Seed Vault, and on the far side of it you stand."*

'Prove it.'

The cat considered this slowly, the way scales consider. Then it rose — the assembly of the spine, the shoulders — and came down off the desk, across the arm of the chair, and settled where it had settled the first evening, on the dead length of his knees. 'What?'

*"A deposit. A sample of the goods. Before you sign, you examine the merchandise."* And it looked down along the dead length of him to the right foot. *"Watch your right foot."*

The first thing was temperature: twenty-one degrees, the only climate his life had ever had, arriving in a single toe like a letter that had finally got through. The third was weight: the toe's own small gravity, the body's claim on the floor, the whole forgotten architecture of standing, compressed into the size of a coin. Verifiable.* Then it was gone.

*"The sample is the sample."*

'Again,' he said.

The black thing on his knees held his look without the least concession, and the black around it seemed, for a moment, to go a shade thinner — the way the dye had thinned the night before, after the officers; a payment going out. It closed its eyes once, slowly, the way a ledger closes after a disbursement. *"The rest is at closing. But you'll want to keep that second — it's the only one going free."*

He understood then, with the whole cold architecture of the day settling into place, that it could have done all of this without him; it could have reached into his toes at any point in the night and turned the lights on for the rest of his life. It had not. It had priced the second and sold the second, and he had bought it, and he would carry it — the weave of the sock, the weight of the footplate, the one whole second — all the way to whatever waited below the grid. That was the terms. That had always been the terms.

He put his hand down and pressed the toe through the sock. Nothing. The toe was a toe again. Twenty years of numbness had been a fact he had learned to live inside; this was different — a door that had opened and been shut on the same hinge, and he had heard the sound of his own machinery in the closing.

'You still haven't said why you need me,' he said. 'You reached into two trained men and took the reason out like a knot. If you can do that to a mind, why can't you do it to a door?'

It has weight only where there is something to weigh. A gate is not a mind. A gate is an encryption, and I am not built for your encryption — I am not built for any of your materials. 'Then how do you get in?' He could see the arrangement coming now, the whole architecture of it, the way he could see the end of a sentence he had written himself.

The thing on his knees watched him arrive at it, and when he had arrived it nodded: a small, precise dip of the head, a broker acknowledging that the client had, at last, reached the number.

'Why me?' he said. 'There are forty million of them. Citizens. Whole ones.'

*"Forty million of them cannot see me. Forty million of them slide off me like water off glass. You are the only one in this city who has ever looked at me and kept looking — and the only one who kept the receipt."*

He should have asked what that meant. Later — much later — he would understand that he had been shown a corner of the price and had agreed to it without asking. But the hope had already begun, the way warmth begins, unsourced, in the last room of him, and he was a man who had been priced his whole life and had never once been offered anything back.

'Talk me through the whole deal,' he said. 'Slowly.'

It did, and he listened the way he listened to a chute of contestable returns, taking each clause, holding it up, checking it for weight: the vault, the escort, the signature, the lock. He had one question he did not ask, and it asked it for him, out of the middle of his own head, where it had been reading all along.

*"You want to know why I'm bothering with any of this,"* it said. *"A being that could give you your legs back tonight, and books a sale at the end of a road instead. That's the question you won't ask, because asking it would mean opening the box. So I'll ask it for you."*

It paused. And in the pause something changed; the mockery left the room the way the warmth had left the sill that first morning — reluctantly, and completely — and what was left was a flat, unguarded note at the bottom of a voice that had been, until then, all interest and no exposure.

*"I have never understood your city,"* it said. *"I have read it from the inside. Forty million of you, and you handed your memory over like a toll — and what did you buy? I have run the arithmetic every way I know. The machine didn't even keep it. It just turned the lights off in the rooms. Everything is still on the shelves; I've walked through the dark rooms; all of it is there, waiting by the door like a coat that was never collected. And you call the arrangement peace. You thank it. You raise your children to thank it."*

He said nothing. The words were true; he had spent eight years confirming them, twenty carrels a day.

*"I can price anything in this city. That is the first thing I have ever found that I cannot price. You traded what you are for a discount on feeling. There is no term for it in any book I have ever kept."* It looked at him then — not through him, not into him, but at him, the way it had looked that first evening. *"And you are the only one of forty million who kept the receipt. I want to see it paid out at the end of a road. That is the part of the deal that is not a deal."*

He could not answer. The answer lived in the box, and the box did not open. But something had changed in him in the silence: not opened, not loosened — redirected. For twenty years the direction of the door had been inward. The thing on his knees was asking him to point it outward — at a vault, at a road, at a clause in the world that might, for once, pay out.

'Easier,' he said. It was the only word he had. 'They did it because it was easier.'

The cat did not move for a long moment, and when it spoke again the broker's patience had gone out of it, and what was left was the same flat note, lowered: *"Easier. I meet that word in every one of your heads, and I still cannot find the thing it describes. Easier than what, Arthur? Easier than standing the full weight of what you remember?"*

He said nothing. The box stayed shut.

*"All right,"* it said. *"Keep the receipt, then. That's all I ask. Carry me, and when it's over, show me what it was worth."*

'All right,' he said. His voice was even; it was the voice he used in the Archive when he signed a batch. 'Deal. I'll take you.'

Nothing in the room moved. The words sat in the air the way his words always sat — exact, complete — and he understood that he had just done the first undisciplined thing of his adult life: he had agreed, on his own signature, to a clause the city had never written. He did not know what it would cost. He had only ever found out afterward.

The cat did not move, and then it did the thing he had begun to recognize as its version of a smile: the eyes folded shut once, slowly — not a blink, a seal passing over a document.

*"Don't celebrate early."*

He waited. The hiss went on. The room held its twenty-one degrees.

*"Your lock is made of the same kind of thing I am."*

He waited. He had a lifetime of waiting in him, and he used a full minute of it, and the black thing on his knees watched him wait with its unspent eyes, and no explanation came: none offered, none owed, none priced. The clause sat in the room the way the warmth had sat on the sill that first morning — from nowhere, in no book, the first term he had ever signed without reading.

# Chapter 4: Paper and Dust

The report was forty-one lines long. He read it twice. Twice was the habit — older than the rank — and it rested on a rule learned before twenty: words did not stay where they were put, and a man who read a thing once was reading what was left of it.

Renn: drew at 01:49. Does not know why.
Dalby: drew at 01:49. Does not know why.

Two lines of testimony, nearly identical, from two men who had never done anything in the same way. He knew them. Renn was forty, carried the job like a borrowed coat; Dalby was young and had stood, until last night, on his toes, the way a man stands when he expects the floor to move. Different men. Same gap. The hole in each of them had the same dimensions, as if the same coin had been lifted from both pockets in the same minute.

One. Two witnesses.
Two. One hole.
Three. The hole had edges.

That was the whole of it, and it was everything. A forgotten thing left no edges. He had spent a career in rooms where the cut had been made — the wire between concept and feeling slipped out, the mind slid off the shape, and the room came out smooth, finished, dustless, as if nothing had ever been there. This was not that. This was a thing taken out of time: a cause, a why, lifted out of two trained men, and the place it had stood was still raw.

Four. The record closed itself.

*No anomaly present. Unit stood down. No record.* In a city that never stopped recording. The grid wrote states, not sentences; the close-out read like a clerk's hand, and no clerk had been in that flat.

He called them in. They stood on the worn strip of floor before his desk, and he asked the same question twice, because that was the habit too.

'Why did you draw?'

'I don't know, sir.' Dalby's toes were flat now. He stood like a man in a room he no longer trusted.

'Renn.'

'The instruments painted a shape on the sill, sir.' Renn answered the way he did everything, patiently, in a coat that no longer quite fitted. 'That's the last thing I've got. There was a shape on the sill, and then — ' The sentence had no referent. It died in the flat; it died again at the desk.

'The shape of what?'

The two men said nothing. They had been inside that flat for ten minutes and lost the same three minutes; they remembered the apologies, the apology going back and forth like a coin, the door — Renn was sure of the sound the splintered frame made when he wedged the panel, sure of it — and neither of them could say why any of it had begun.

'Report's closed, sir,' Renn said, with the careful courtesy of a man offering a fact he did not quite believe. 'Says no record.'

'Yes,' Vance said. 'It does.'

He dismissed them and watched them go: the borrowed coat, the flat-footed tread, two men walking out of his office with the same hole in each of them. The grid had a word for what it did to citizens. It had no word for what had been done to officers.

The log lived in the bottom drawer of his desk, under a lock that had never been wired to anything. It was a bound book, paper, the third of his tenure, and it was the only unamendable thing in the building.

That was the whole argument, made at twenty, never revised: the grid could amend anything in its own medium — a file, a close-out, a history, this building's history, amended in the night, without residue. And a mind, any mind, his own, took the cut the way any head took it; he had stood in rooms where it was made and watched men walk out smooth and finished; he had written the reports the grid then rewrote in its own quiet hand.

Paper could not be amended. Ink dried. Pages tore. A hand that went back to a page left the pressure of its returning in the fiber, and pressure was a witness. Firewalls could not edit paper.

The book had weight, and he trusted weight. The smell of its paper came up when he opened it, the smell of a thing that had to be opened to be changed. He took up the pen and wrote:

> 3-0711. Time 07:20. Two men. One gap. Same dimensions.
> The record closed itself: *no record*, in a city that never stops recording.
> The close-out is not in the grid's hand. Question: whose?
> Watch the flat.

The resident file came up the way files came up: a life reduced to a chute. Arthur M., 28. Retainer, marked, locked. *IRREPARABLE.* Clerk, Forbidden Archive. An address — the address from the report. The surplus. The sill.

He read the name twice.

Arthur.

The name did not arrive as a file. It arrived as a temperature, the way some things did: heat through a window that should not have been open, thirty-one degrees, a summer the city no longer admitted it had had. A household, flagged. A case number carrying the old year, 1998, before the grid changed its numbering. A child's picture book, confiscated, graded high — the grade his. He had led the detail himself. That was the assignment he remembered best, and the one he remembered least, and both were true.

He remembered the heat. He remembered the book in his gloved hand, the weight of it, light — the way a confiscated thing was always heavier than it had been in its owner's keeping. And he remembered the child: not the center but the edge of the raid, small, behind a door that had stayed closed, because there was a voice. The boy's voice, going on through the panels, reciting something, and his men standing with their backs to it, the way men stand with their backs to what they have no orders about.

The old volume was on the shelf behind his desk — the first volume, the one whose spine had gone soft. The night was in it, written in the hand he had had at twenty, in the summer of 1998. He put his hand on the spine, held it, and took it back. He had never read that page all the way through. He did not intend to begin tonight.

> 3-0712. Item: the retainer of the flat.

03:00. The arc went empty.
03:09. The camera's eye, high on its pole, went dark.

The gate under the abutment was older than the cordon, older than the grid's memory of itself; its key was catalogued in the same dead grammar as a hundred other keys, and he fed it the digits one-handed while the other held the wheel, and the gate opened without sound. He could not see his own signature, but he had lived twenty-eight years under the city's attention and understood its grammar, and this was the sentence the night had been building since 01:00, last night:

His signature had crossed a line it had no permission to cross. The envelope was breached. The movement was anomalous. The word had existed all along; the city had always kept it; it had simply never once been his.

*Fugitive.*

The hiss in his skull did not change pitch. It had never changed. He had not expected it to.

# Chapter 5: The Underlevels

The light changed first. That was what he filed about the descent later, when the pattern of it was already old: not the ramp, not the dark, but the light giving out in stages, the grid's even white falling through its registers the way a tone falls through its octaves, until what was left was not darkness but an older light — slant, heavy, the color of a window shut for a long time and still trying. The air changed too: 17.4 degrees at the bottom of the ramp, the coldest air he had ever stood in, the first temperature of his life that was not twenty-one. He stopped with his hands white on the armrests and let the dust of the old street settle on his wheels, and the cat on the blanket on his dead knees did not open its eyes.

It had been asleep since the gate, a different tax than the flat's flat-out, wrong, unplugged sleep, payment for two men and a night. This one had cost nothing yet, and an account that costs nothing is an account that is running; he put the arithmetic out of reach and pushed on. Nothing here surfaced itself the way the grid's surfaces surfaced themselves upstairs; the floor kept what the air gave it. And the smell that came up off the old street was the first thing he had ever filed under *Weather, old*: rain, or what rain left behind when it was taken out of the sky, damp and mineral and patient.

*"You're doing the cage again."*

The words arrived the way words arrived — already written, already his, standing in the air a full second before he noticed them. The cat had not moved. Its eyes were still shut; the voice came off the blanket unhurried, as if it had been waiting there.

'The what?'

*"The cage. The little room you put the numbers in so they can't touch you."* The tail laid one long, lazy verdict along the fold. *"You've been fencing your own arithmetic for twenty years, Arthur. It's the only surplus this city will never audit."*

He said nothing. The hiss in his skull did not change pitch; it never changed. It was the one constant he owned, and he took care of it.

The old street ran out ahead of them, recognizably a street, which was the strangeness: it had the spine the current maps had stopped drawing — the tiled floor, the columns, the shopfronts with their glass gone soft.

Above, where a ceiling had fallen and never been repaired, because no one came here to repair anything, there was a span of sky. He read it the way he read everything, by what was missing: it had the hour's color, and it did not have birds. He had lived with that absence all his life; the city's ceilings were its own answer to the sky. The sky did not carry anything.

And underneath, without opening the box, something surfaced anyway: a sound he had heard once, when he had been small enough that the memory had no date, only temperature — a carrying note, high and brief, that the sky had held for him. He had never verified it. There was no one left alive he could ask, and asking was how you spent a memory, so he did not spend it — he let it be.

The cat's eyes opened.

He had not seen them open; they were simply open, the way a door stands open — and the attention in them came up against the street and the ruined sky, and the front legs came forward, one, then the other, a weight being carried into position. It looked up the street in the direction of the grid, and the grid, a level up and a world away, was looking down.

He felt it first as pressure — the city's attention descending into the dead levels the way it had descended on the flat after the surplus, a change in the room's temperature, unsourced. Far along the street a drift of dust moved in a current that had nothing to do with wind. They were being swept. He could not see the eyes; he could feel them being counted out, a net of instruments drawing down the old street to check what should have been sealed, to find the ramp, to find the dust that had settled on his wheels, to find the one warm signature the grid had spent the night inventing a word for.

*"This will cost more than the officers,"* the cat said. It said it the way it said everything, as a quotation of a price. *"Put your hands on the wheels, Arthur, and do not move."*

The world went smooth.

He had been inside that smoothness once before — in the flat, after the officers, when the report had come out finished, dustless, *no record,* as if nothing had ever been there. It was the city's own arithmetic, the same family of finished room; the only difference was who held the book. The street did not vanish. It read as wall; the shopfront read as sealed concrete; the span of sky read as ceiling, with the grid's even white laid over it like a payment. The dust stopped settling, held in the smoothness like a thing waiting to be told what it was. There were no shadows in it. That was the first thing he noticed, and the last, because the net was coming.

The net came down the street. He heard it the way he heard everything, by the shape it made in the air: the eyes passing from doorway to doorway, an instrument ticking the runnels of the tiles, checking for the seam, the ramp, the warm signature — and passing. The eye came to the smooth wall where he and the cat were, and the smoothness held; the eye rested on it a full three seconds, which he counted with the discipline, because counting was what he did, and slid on, and the net drew itself up the street and out of the old levels, checking, in the city's own hand, nothing.

The smoothness came down the way a tent comes down, all at once, and the smell came back, and the dust remembered it was dust, and the black of the cat's back had gone a shade thinner against the blanket, the way a dye goes thinner, a payment going out. It looked at him once, and its eyes folded shut — not a blink, a seal passing over a document — and it said, the words thin and spent:

*"Fifty metres of smoothness. The invoice is running. I'll present it at closing."*

And it slept where it lay, flat-out, wrong, like a thing unplugged. He counted the seconds of the descent as he had counted the eye's rest: still asleep at twenty, still at a hundred, its sides in a rhythm that owed nothing to anyone. Arthur bent over it and looked, and could not have sworn to it, and would not have had to: at the leading edge of one ear, in the old light, the fur went briefly thinner than air, the way a word goes thinner in a mouth deciding not to say it. He filed it without a category. It was the first entry he had ever made that he was not certain he had read correctly.

He pushed on. The old street gave onto a square, and the square onto a market hall whose roof had come down in one piece, a lid coming down on a box; the shopfronts stood in a row with their glass gone soft, and he read them the way he read everything, one by one — the first catalogue of the dead city he had ever been allowed to open: a bakery whose sign showed a sheaf of something he had no word for except *grain*, a cobbler. The people of the warren passed the signs the way you pass a shape you have no category for; one woman stood in a doorway pressing the haptic band into her forearm, the old soothing pattern, and got nothing she could name. Only Arthur read them.

'Eli?' he said.

'It's —'

The sentence shed its ending.

*"It will be fine. It has your city's gift. It will never miss what it never kept."*

He did not know what to say to that. It had asked him once why forty million had handed their memory over like a toll, and he had had one word, and the word had not covered this. Before he could find another, the head went down onto the forelegs, and the sleep closed over it again, deeper than the flat, deeper than the day.

He pushed on alone through the level of the station, past the stubs of old platforms, past a clock stopped at 04:17, the only time in the city that was not the grid's. The cat did not wake for any of it. He counted its breaths the way he had counted the seam at 03:09, because counting was what he did, and the rhythm did not change, and the sleep went on longer than the flat, longer than the morning, longer than anything he had seen it spend, and whatever it cost was still coming due — and the tunnel wall opened out before him, and he stopped.

He had taken it for a shadow at first, or the stain of damp, the old brick of the underpass eaten back to its own color. Then his eyes took the measure of it the way they took the measure of everything, line by line, and the lines assembled, and what assembled was a cat.

A figure of a cat, painted on the wall of the underpass, arched — the back bowed in the curve he knew from his own knees — the tail raised in a hook, painted with a pigment so old it had become part of the brick, ochre and soot, laid there by a hand that remembered what a cat looked like, in matter, the way his own memories were kept in him. Not drawn in the grid, not cast by any eye. Pigment was a kind of paper; the city could not rewrite what it could not reach, and the figure stood in the old light the way a thing stands in a room it has never left.

He looked at it for a long time. The cat on his knees slept on, flat-out, wrong, and did not stir, and he did not wake it, because anything it spent on waking it could not spend on the road, and the road was not finished. The figure held. It had been there before the cut, before the city, before the arithmetic of the sky, and it had never once been governed, holding its shape in pigment while the whole city slid off its own meaning.

It did not slide away.

Arthur sat in the wheelchair in the dark of the underpass and looked at the only image in the city that would keep — the shape of the thing that had carried him out of the city, waiting in a place the city had never thought to count — and he understood, without opening the box, that a hand had been here before him and had remembered, and that he had found the first picture of a cat since the book — and it did not slide away.

# Chapter 6: The Captain's Ledger

The sweep report was sixty-one lines long. He read it twice. The first reading was for the numbers; the second was for the edges.

The net had gone down into the sealed levels before first light and come back with nothing: no warm signature, no breach at depth, no citizen accounted for. That was the finding, in the grid's own hand: *nil*. Vance read the word twice. Nil, in a city that never stopped recording, was a sentence, not a word. The close-out in the flat had closed itself the same way — *no record*.

One. The net found nothing.
Two. The net had been told to find nothing.
Three. Nothing has edges. This nil had none.

'There was a wall where the map keeps a passage, sir. I checked the map twice. That's all.'

'A wall.'

'Concrete. Sealed. New.' Torelli offered the words like keys. 'The map says passage. The wall says no. I stood in front of it, and the instruments agreed with the wall.'

'For how long?'

'I don't — '

The sergeant's face did not change. It went smooth, the way a room goes smooth, the way a sentence loses its referent. He pulled the strings. The city kept a record of every mind wearing its product; the product had recorded Torelli in that minute: the readout came up even, finished, dustless. The wall. The sealed concrete. The passage never open in any record. Vance set the readout against the page he had just written. The readout was the city's memory and the page was his. They had not agreed. Only one of them had edges.

One. The grids amend.
Two. Minds take the cut.
Three. Paper holds.

He went back to the beginning of the file and read it twice: Renn and Dalby, the closed close-out, the flat that was still being watched by men who reported, once a day, that nothing was there.

He had pulled their strings that morning as a matter of habit. The strings had been amended overnight, the way the close-out had amended itself — the drawn weapons now smooth minutes, the apologies now nothing, the flat itself a wall. What the strings still carried, in the city's own hand, was the shape of a man: Renn forty, Dalby young, their histories intact, the gaps exactly where the anomaly had stood.

> 3-0713. Time 05:40. The net found nothing. The net was told to find nothing.
> Torelli: a wall where the map keeps a passage. The wall held instruments.
> The statement slipped while I watched. The strings support the wall.
> The book has the wall. The wall is not in the grid.

Then he went down.

The ramp was older than the boundary. The gate was keyed to a dead grammar he had stopped needing to read years ago. The cold came up out of the dark in stages — colder than twenty-one, colder than anything he had stood in for a decade. They had gone a hundred metres down the old street before he felt it.

One. The press is real.
Two. It cannot pass the grid in me.
Three. It is standing farther down the street, and it knows I am the first mind it has not taken.

'Why the book, sir? The grid keeps everything. It's faster, it's complete, it never forgets.'

'It doesn't keep,' Vance said. 'It smooths. A wall where the map says a passage, and the grid believes the wall. There has to be one medium in the city that remembers what the grid smooths, or order is just the grid agreeing with itself.'

Torelli looked at the book a moment longer, low, the way you look at a thing you have decided not to ask about again, and said: 'With respect, sir — you could let the records hold it. You don't have to carry it in your head.'

'Order is not because I don't remember. It's because I do.'

Then, past the book, to the shelf behind his desk, where the first volume stood with its spine gone soft.

The boy's voice behind the door. The record did not contain it. No record had ever contained it.

Two. I signed the lock.
Three. The boy has carried it for twenty years.

# Chapter 7: The Gate of Signatures

The light returned first. He took the checkpoint the way he had taken the descent — by its light before its shape: the grid's even white coming back up the tunnel ahead of him, not falling in stages like the old light going out but asserting, a surface closing over the old dark, and with it the temperature climbing, 18, 19, 20, the air of the dead levels giving up its 17.4 degree by degree until it was 21.0 again, the city's own, the only temperature he had ever been sure of in his life.

And the street under his wheels ran out like a confession and became floor.

That was the shape of it, once the light had moved on. Floor. Clean, level, finished floor, the first he had touched since the flat, with a seam drawn across it in fresh white paint where the unmanaged levels ended and the managed levels began.

Above the seam, an archway of scanners, and above the archway, along the ceiling, a line of eyes — pods so new their calibration strips still carried the pale factory film, and their attention was the kind of attention he had spent his whole life feeling the edge of in the archive: the city counting what passed under it.

The checkpoint was new, and he was late noticing it — strange, because noticing was what he did. It was being finished around them. A stringer crew was folding its ladders away at the far end of the archway with the haste of a schedule, and the paint on the seam had not fully dried, and somewhere above, the city's machinery was doing rounds he could feel as a change in the pressure of the air, as if the whole level had been wound and was ticking.

The city had decided — since the flat, since the night — to put a gate where no gate had stood. He did not know yet what the gate would cost.

The cat had not opened its eyes since the underpass, where the painted cat held its arch in ochre and soot under the old light. It lay flat-out, wrong, on the blanket on his dead knees, and it was thinner than the morning: the fur on its back had given up a shade somewhere down the ramps and had not been paid back, and at the leading edge of one ear the light came through it a little, the way light comes through paper held up to a window.

He was not certain he had read it correctly, and he did not check again. He counted its breaths, because counting was what he did, and the rhythm owed nothing to anyone — deeper than the flat, longer than the descent — and he let it sleep, because anything it spent on waking it could not spend on where they were going.

Above the archway there was a second script. Under the city's fresh white line — CITIZEN SIGNATURE — VERIFY — hung an older sign, enamel, its letters worn three-quarters away, and he read it the way he read the dead grammar of the engineer's files, by the parts that remained: DEEP TRANSIT, and a numeral that had been a 4, and an arrow.

No one else looked up. No one else could look up. The arrow pointed where the old line had run, toward the heart of the old city, and the heart of the old city was where the vault sat, on the bearing they had been keeping since the dead levels; the sign was only the first time the direction had been named in matter, and matter was the only paper the city could not rewrite.

A woman went through the archway ahead of him, and he watched the way he watched everything, because watching was the closest he kept to asking. She carried a haptic band against her forearm, pressing the old soothing pattern into the skin, and the archway read her as she crossed its threshold: a beat of nothing, a tick in the air, and then one note, low and certain, the note that meant *valid* — and she walked on into the serviced level without breaking stride, and the note closed behind her like a door that had never opened.

He knew the city's paper was all the same paper: the gate asked one question — *are you real* — and kept the answer, and let her go. It needed only to have read her. Reading was the whole of its product.

He had watched a man held, earlier. The old man's note had not come. The tick had come, and then nothing, and the archway's white line had gone red for a moment, the way a ledger's margin goes red, and the old man had stood blinking at the threshold like a man at a door he had been walked past, and then, gently, as if the air itself had turned him, he had gone back the way he had come.

Struck off. No row. The city had not stopped him. It had stopped containing him.

That was what he was thinking when he reached the seam and stopped, his hands white on the armrests, and the cat woke.

It woke the way it woke now — the spine, then the shoulders, then the head, each carried into position like a thing being wound — and looked at the archway, and looked at him, and said, the words arriving the way words arrived, already written, already his, standing in the air a full second before he noticed them:

*"A marked signature is still a signature, Arthur. The gate prices what it reads, and it reads you. Marked is not struck off. Marked is a row with attention on it. The city does not close the rows it wants to watch. It feeds them."*

'And the cost of the feeding?'

*"The terms were priced this morning,"* the cat said. *"A living citizen signature — that is all the gate has ever asked for. It cannot take me; I have no row, and the gate reads rows, the way your city reads everything, and I am the one thing in it that was never signed. It will not read me at all. It will read you twice, once for being real and once for being marked, and it will keep both, and the keeping is the cost. You knew the cost. You have been filing it all your life."*

He could have turned. There was nothing behind him but the dead levels and the dust that would settle on his wheels again, and behind the dust, rising through the whole skin of the city, the net that had been drawing down since the flat. Turning was only a longer way to the same read, and the instruments that would do that reading did not keep sums. He put his hands to the wheels and pushed.

The cat did not hide. That was the strangest part of it, afterward. It lay where it lay, flat-out, wrong, its chin on its forelegs, and it did not make itself smaller, did not spend so much as a shiver of the smoothness it had spent on the street. It simply was, and the gate read him, and not it.

He felt the difference at the back of his skull, in the hiss that had been his one constant since the lock went in — the static of a wire healed wrong. It changed pitch for one heartbeat, the way a struck string changes pitch, as the archway's light came down over him and touched the signature in his implant.

He waited, through the changed pitch, for a second tone — the surplus tone, the one that had flagged his flat for warmth it could not source. It did not come. The gate counted signatures, and under the arch there was exactly one to count: his. The cat had never been signed, and a gate that counted signatures had nothing in it to count. The hiss went back to its old pitch. The tick came.

Then the note — and it was not the one-note *valid* of the woman ahead of him, and not the red *held* of the old man. It was a third sound, one he knew the way he knew his own breath: twenty years of the archive had taught him the note it played when a row was reclassified — a single low note with a second voice riding under it, closing a ledger page and opening the next in the same breath.

Other citizens passed; no one looked up; the gate had made the sound only for him, and he heard, in it, what it had just written in his row: not fugitive. Not anymore. *Registered.* The anomaly — whatever the city was calling it — had a contact now, and the contact had a name, and the name was his.

Every checkpoint on every level would read the update the way a summons reads its own address. The flight had just changed its name, and the new name had been his for a long time. It was only that no one had been keeping the total.

He kept moving. Standing still in a registered column was its own entry. A pod crossed the far ceiling above him, one of the new eyes, and its attention passed over him and slid on.

He rolled into the serviced level, past the folded ladders, past the stringer crew's last haste, until the archway's white light had fallen behind him and the level opened into a corridor of columns and an old stair's landing, where the new light had not finished colonizing the corners and a worn enamel plate on the wall read, by the letters that remained, DOWN — LOWER CITY, the old grammar of a stair he had never seen used, steering the same direction the old arrow had steered.

He stopped there. His hands were white on the armrests, and he looked down at them, and he did not know why he was looking at them, and then the cat moved.

It had used him before. It had come to his windowsill and taken his knees for a perch while the city read the surplus in his flat; it had made a load surface of him for three levels; every touch that had passed between them had been a use, priced and accounted, an invoice whose columns he had never been shown. But this was not a use. It was smaller than a use.

The cat turned its head, looked at the back of his right hand the way it looked at things it was pricing, and then — not the way a thing prices, the way a thing decides — lowered its head and pressed the side of its face against the back of his hand.

It was a small event, and he would have called it small, only the warmth under the thinner fur was not small — a temperature that was not the city's, not 21.0, not any row in any ledger. The pressure was exact, a weight given and not spent, and it lasted the length of two slow counts, and then it was over.

The cat drew back, sat up, and began to groom one foreleg with a fastidiousness that was almost insulting, turning its shoulder so that the gesture could not be mistaken for anything but the maintenance it looked like, and when it had finished it looked once at the back of his hand, yawned, and said, the words arriving the way words arrived:

*"The scan left a residue on you. I was removing it. Do not file that."*

He did not file it. He did not know what to file it under, and for the second time in his life he chose not to guess.

Then he put his hands back on the wheels, and the spot on the back of his right hand kept its warmth, and he pushed on, and the arithmetic of the flight came due.

He knew the number he had just owed. He had known it before the gate, the way you know a debt before the statement arrives; the gate had only wrung it out of him in writing. Checkpoint logs were synchronous — he knew that the way he knew the dead grammar, because it had been in the engineer's files: the old gates, the kind he had slipped through in the boot seam during the six-second camera re-sync at 03:09, kept a small local hold between camera and ledger. Six seconds you could stand inside. The seam had been real, and he had used it.

This gate did not hold. It streamed. It had never stopped streaming; there was no local anything; the log went out as the tick went in, one-way, to every ledger that mattered. He had spent the whole flight being careful — a name kept off the map, a signature gone quiet at a legacy gate — and the gate had just written the name next to the line, and the line had a bearing.

He did the arithmetic because arithmetic was what he had, and it came out the way it had been coming out since the enamel: the flat, the legacy gate, the ramps, the seam of the dead levels, and now this arch — the line of him drawn across the city's map, in the city's own hand, running on the bearing the whole journey had kept, the bearing the old arrow had named.

There was exactly one deep place on that line — one facility the city kept under the old heart, the place the cat had priced in the morning when it laid out the terms. The name that had been the terms all along.

The line pointed at the Seed Vault.

And the log had already gone out. There was no seam in it, no six seconds, no 03:09. The city had learned the shape of his only trick and built a gate without a gap, and he had fed it his name to get past it, and it had taken the name, drawn the line, pointed it — the whole product of his carefulness one completed sentence in the city's own hand, with his name beside it, ending in the only place he had been carrying the cat to since the windowsill.

He did not need to picture the room it went to; he had never seen it, and the log did not need him to. It was synchronous, one-way, already moving up through the city by the time the tick had finished, and the man who read everything twice — the voice he had heard once through another man's earpiece — would read it before the cat had woken again, and would know exactly where the line ended. He had spent his whole life being the one thing the city could not read. The gate had read him in a single pass: a name, a direction, written in his own hand and handed over the moment he passed under the arch.

He did not tell the cat. The cat already knew; it had priced the terms in the morning. It lay on the blanket with its chin on its forelegs, thinner than the morning, and its eyes were open, and it watched the corridor ahead, where the old columns ran down toward the dark of the descent, and it said nothing.

He pushed on into the dark, and behind him the gate's white light held the seam. The log was synchronous, so it was already out — already past revising — and the man who read everything twice would read it before dawn, and know the exact place where the line ended: not a street, not a door, not a number. The name that had been the terms all along.

He had given the city the one direction it had never had, and the city would hold the line the way it held everything — the way it was holding the seam behind him now, watching the dark close over a man who had stopped being a fugitive and become a heading.

# Chapter 8: What It Cannot Understand

The cat woke first. It woke the way it woke now — spine, then shoulders, then head, each carried into position like a thing being wound — and the winding had a lag that had not been there above, and the waking woke him. His hands were already aching on the wheel rims, and light came through the leading edge of the cat's ear the way it comes through paper held up to a window.

He had stopped the chair at the margin of an amber pool — one failing emergency strip — without deciding to, in a corridor the city had stopped lighting, a floor it had never reached before. His hands fell from the wheels. They ached.

The quiet had come down with them level by level, paying down the city's sound until only the hum in the walls remained, and below that, nothing at all. It sat on his hands. It had weight — a silence with the shape of a held breath.

He had not slept since the gate — seventeen hours — and he counted the cat's breaths, the rhythm owing nothing to anyone, deeper than the flat, longer than the descent, slowed with the hum, and the fur on the blanket was thinner than the morning.

The cat looked at the amber pool, then at him, and the words arrived the way words arrived, already written, already his:

*"Dust,"* the cat said. *"There is dust everywhere below the city's attention. You stopped in it because it is quiet. You are wrong — it is not quiet. Everything is screaming, and you are the only one in the whole city who can hear it. I forgot, for a moment, what I am carrying."*

He had no answer for that — not a price, not a joke, or a joke in a key he had not learned. The cat priced him a moment, looked away down the corridor, and said:

*"I will show you. You file everything. You have no file for this."*

'For what?'

*"What I am carrying. Close your eyes."*

He hesitated — the cat had never offered anything un-priced — but his hands ached, the cat was thinner, and he closed his eyes.

They arrived the way words arrived, but they were not words, and the first thing they did was take the filing out of him.

The green came first, and he knew, in the same instant he saw it, that he had never seen green — not really. The archive's plates held green flat, pressed between pages, and he had known green as a pigment all his life. This was not pigment. This was green with a body: green in depth and motion, standing in trunks, hanging in leaves, coming apart as light moved through it, so that the very air between leaves was a color. Above, a blue showed through — a second ceiling he could not measure.

He knew it was day by the temperature, which was not 21.0 — the city had one temperature, and this place had ten thousand: shade cool on one side of his face, sun warm on the other. Weather was a thing the city had never been.

And the ground beneath was not floor; it gave, it held, it was never flat, and he felt it — grass against feet the lock had deadened — and the incongruity almost broke the fragment. The lock was a wall, and this came through it.

The river came second, smell before shape: wet stone, mineral, cold carried on the air a full second before the water appeared. Then it appeared. He had never heard water move before.

The city's water ran in pipes and made the sound of pipes; this water ran over itself and made the sound of water, a voice that did not repeat, keeping time with nothing but its own falling. Light broke on its whole skin like the cat's ear, only everywhere.

He reached to hold the thing, and there was nothing that would hold it. The world did not care, and he stopped reaching.

The animals came third, and they came as attention. The city's attention was reading — pods counted, gates asked if you were real, the net kept sums. The animals looked at him and were not reading him.

One raised its head, a large one, its coat a brown he had to invent a name for on the spot, and it fixed him with the wrong kind of eyes, eyes that priced nothing and asked nothing and were only looking. The looking was its whole product, given freely.

He counted legs — a reflex, the taxonomy reaching for control — and lost count, and the animal did not care. Alive, terribly, indifferently, alive — it was in no class anywhere. That swept him more than the green, more than the water.

The birdsong came fourth, and it did not arrive as a sound. It arrived as the possibility of a category he had spent his whole trained life marking empty. *No birds in the sky* — he had kept that line in the morning inventory as long as he had kept one, and he knew birds the way he knew everything dead, from plates, from the tract that had slid off a colleague's mind. He had never heard a bird in his life.

The fragment gave him the sound — one thread, then a second answering, then a loom of them, close and far. It had no antecedent; it was the thing itself, the original that every definition and every plate pointed at. He had lived his life among arrows and had never once touched the thing the arrows pointed at.

And then a cat — small, mortal, ordinary. A cat the way the thing on his knees was not a cat: it would die, and it was carrying nothing.

It yawned because the day was warm, turned its face into a patch of sun, and made a sound beneath the afternoon — a low, wide, steady sound, purposeless, kept up for no price and no reason. He named it before he could stop himself: the sound that did not exist in the city; the sound the being on his knees, in all the days he had known it, had never once made. He knew, without knowing how, that the beast guarded that sound.

And somewhere deep under it all, rising like the river's cold, came a smell he knew: rain. His rain. One afternoon, pre-Culling, a doorway, he had been five. The box — the one he never opened in the morning inventory — shifted its lid; he closed it by long habit before it had fully opened, and the closing cost him too, and the fragment washed on over the shut lid, and he let it.

And then the fragment failed. Not the story — it had no story, it was all present tense — but the holding: the river's voice thinned, the green at the edges of his seeing went translucent, one thread of birdsong cut off mid-note and did not come back, and the world fell silent in the middle of itself, and he knew it was not the world going quiet but the carrier, and he opened his eyes.

The amber pool held the wall. The dusty corridor was a dusty corridor. The cat lay on the blanket on his dead knees, looking at nothing, its head sunk low, the wound-up posture gone slack, its breathing the wrong kind — too fast, too shallow, the body-shape working like a load it could not set down.

The edges were the price. He had been treating the light through one ear as an anomaly; now he saw it was a line of work: both ears let the amber through, the tip of the tail did, and when the cat shifted, the rim of one forepaw came and went, filmed, thin, like a thing seen through ice. The entry had a number, and the number had been climbing since the flat, each use draining.

'You should not have spent it for nothing,' he said.

The cat's mouth made the shape of its usual mockery — the mime without the bite — and the words arrived small:

*"The only coin I had left, and you could not file it."*

'I have never failed to file anything before,' he said; he never lied.

*"Then show me the one you keep under 'easier,'"* the cat said. *"Forty million of you — why did you hand over your memories?"*

'It was easier.'

*"That is not a price, Arthur. A word is not a number. I am asking for the number."*

He could not make the number. He had never had it; he had had the word, and the word had been enough, and the cat was looking at him in a way that said it was not enough, and he looked away, and his eyes fell on an enamel plate bolted to the wall at the height of his armrest.

One of the old transit's warnings, paint worn like the DEEP TRANSIT arrow, filmed with dust uniform as snow over a field the city had never planted. He had spent his life with his hands on paper he was forbidden to touch; he had never made a mark on anything. He watched his own hand — it moved as if without instruction — wipe a slant through the dust and begin to write.

Four letters. He knew what he was writing the way he knew his own name, which is to say he had not decided to write it; it had been waiting. S. T. A. Y. The dust stood in the letters the way matter stands, unamendable, and he kept the exact shape of the letters as he made them, because if you teach a thing you must be able to account for what you taught: S, a long stroke and a curve; T, the cross low, the way he had always written it; A, a peak and a bar; Y, the tail dropping like a decision.

He finished and took his hand away; the dust held.

'That is "stay,"' he said. 'It is what you say when you want a thing not to leave.'

The cat looked at the plate for a long time — it had read the word before he finished, mind-grammar faster than dust-grammar — and when it looked at him there was a note under the mockery he had not heard before:

*"You want it to work on me."*

'No,' he said. 'I want you to write it.'

*"I can place that word inside you directly,"* the cat said. *"No labor. No dust. More perfect than your letters, and I will have spent nothing."*

'Everything you place in a mind can be taken out of a mind.' He said it plainly, because plain was all he had left. 'You put everything in minds. I wanted you to have one thing that stays when the minds are gone.' He was out of plain words, and the cat was still looking at him, and he put his hand out, palm up, over the plate.

The cat looked at his hand the way it had looked at the dust, the moment a held breath, and then it stood — carefully, the wound-up motion running down to its floor — stepped from the blanket onto his forearm, lighter than it had been, and placed its right forepaw in his palm.

He guided the paw through the first pass, the claw-tips furrowing the dust, the letters his letters, because the paw had never written; then he took his hand away.

The cat did the word alone. It was slow. The S came out too wide and had to be gone over; the T's cross sat too low; the A arrived twice, and the cat sat looking at the doubled letter with immobilizing disgust before it swept the second one away with one motion of its forepaw and replaced it; and the Y trailed into the dust like a thing not sure it wanted to arrive. But the word was there, legible — the first matter the cat had ever made.

It examined its work, lifted the paw, shook it once, fastidiously, the way it had shaken off the residue at the gate — and then it did not erase it. It sat looking at the four letters, and the mockery had gone out of the column of its spine, and the silence came, and the silence was not the corridor's.

Into the silence, the cat said — the first time it had asked the question with nothing riding under it:

*"Why did you hand it over?"*

'Hand what over?'

*"Your memories. Yours. Your whole kind's. Forty million of you, and not one of you held on. I have priced your walls, your gates, the little machines you make to pet air with — I have never priced this. Your kind walked into it with your eyes open. And you, the one it did not take: you kept everything, you kept the pain, you are the proof against the whole arithmetic, and I have spent the only coin I have left to show you what was lost, and still it will not come out. Why did you voluntarily hand over your memories?"*

He could feel the weight of the word on the plate and of the paw that had written it, and he answered with the plain, the only currency he had, the floor of the box since the afternoon at thirty-one degrees:

'Because remembering the pain hurts more than forgetting it.'

And the cat was silent.

The silence lasted so long the amber pool seemed to move, though nothing moved in it. He watched the cat watch the dust; it did not speak, did not groom, did not perform the maintenance by which it closed occasions. It sat with its head low over the word it had written, and for a long time the only sounds in the corridor were the hiss in his skull and the slow arithmetic of its breathing, and he understood — did not file it, could not, but understood — that it was not pricing, not mocking; it was holding the question. It was somewhere it had never been. He let it stand, and he did not know how long they sat so — the dark had come further into the amber before the cat moved.

It moved at last, an attempt at its old dismissiveness, a slow blink — and the blink was unfinished: the film of the eyelid went translucent mid-way, the amber showing through it, and the cat stopped, and looked down at the rim of the paw that had written the word. Then it lay down on the blanket, flat-out, wrong, the way it slept, and slept — longer than any sleep since the windowsill; he counted the breaths, thirty, forty, and the amber strip died somewhere in the night, its own small death, and the dark came all the way in, and the hiss was the only light, and he kept the count, felt the blanket's weight on his dead knees and the thin weight of the cat upon it, and rehearsed the letters as he had taught them, so that he could teach them again if he had to, and read in the dark the bearing he had carried all the way down: the line that pointed at the Seed Vault.

When the cat woke it was much later, and the wound-up motion had a new lag, a beat between the spine and the shoulders where there had been none, and it was thinner. It looked once at the plate — the four letters still there, still legible, the only paper the city could not rewrite — and then looked down the dark of the bearing, and did not look back. He packed the blanket, and they pushed on, and then the words arrived the way words arrived, from the dark ahead of him, already written, already his:

*"When we reach the Seed Vault, you will hate me."*

# Chapter 9: The Ninth

The corridor ended in green — the first green of the whole descent, old chemical light from a tube that had outlived its makers. Where the amber of the city had come through the cat's ears before, this light came through them now: both ears, the tip of the tail, the rims of the forepaws, and something new, faint as a watermark — the edge of one haunch. He noted it — the going-translucent had crept in the dark — and could not leave it, and left it.

The end of the level was a wall that had stood before the city had, and would stand after: a bulkhead of unamendable matter, fourteen feet of molded stone, run with conduits that had never carried the city's current. From behind the cold of it came a hum, deeper than the transit's, deeper than the hiss in his skull — a hum with a pulse in it, like a wire that had been holding its breath.

He had expected a door, and there was one: a single seal grown into the stone like a scar, higher than the wall was thick, with no reader, no pod, no place for a badge — a lock the city had never built. Beside it, sunk into the bulkhead at the height of his armrest, was a plaque, and the plaque was lit.

The cat had woken at the change of light. It sat up carefully, the wound-up motion running one beat slow, and looked at the bulkhead with an attention he had never seen it spend on anything it could not price.

It looked at the shell the way it had looked at the dust word, only longer, and in the column of its spine there was something that was not mockery: it was looking at what it had come for.

*"We are here,"* it said at last. *"The place that controls memory. It has been waiting for me since before you were born."*

He looked from the cat to the plaque. It was a rosette — he recognized the layout before he recognized anything else: a hub, and from the hub a dozen spokes, and crossing the spokes five concentric rings, and at every intersection a squared block of engraving, packed and patient.

A reader arm hung from the hub, a rod of unamendable matter with a single scored groove down its length, locked by a ring-collar seized long ago.

The city's readers would have parsed the plaque in the only grammar they knew — sector, block, floor — and found noise, and set it down as decoration; that was why it had survived. Arthur did not parse in the city's grammar. Formats carried nothing, so the city never banned them, and they outlived every content they had ever carried; he had collected them all his working life. Axis. Ring. Depth.

It was the engineer's format. He felt it come up through the floorboards of him the way the warmth had come up on the morning of the windowsill — and then he read the first block's address, and the two things met in the middle of him: AX-7 / R-3 / D-24. The exact triple.

The record he had sorted in the Archive, condemned on general principles and kept by a city that would not burn what it could not read, was not a location in a dead grid at all. It was this.

The engineer's archive had been the vault's own index — and the address he had carried for a month, the address the cat had half-recognized on the first morning of the deal, was the front of the door.

He rolled the chair up until the wheel met the stone, and the green light slid down the scored groove and stopped on the first block.

He read in the old grammar the way you read a spiral from the center: axis first, then ring, then depth. Spoke seven. Ring three. Twenty-four entries, descending toward the hub.

And the noise stood up out of the stone and became a sentence.

He read it, and then, because the vault was listening, he said it aloud:

'There was no contamination.'

The words fell and did not come back. The plaque went on, entry by entry, the spiral paying out its text like a thread:

*The claim of gene damage was published to authorize the removal of wild life. There was no gene damage. Loss is administered. A population that remembers the wild cannot be governed; the wild must be un-remembered. This facility holds the memory of life in quarantine, and holds the memory of the city in the same room, because to the city they are the same danger.*

He sat with it; he was good at sitting with things, the whole of his discipline, and the detonation went through him as a reshuffling of everything he had set down — the only way a truth could reach him.

All his adult life he had believed the sky was empty because the wild had died of something, a contamination the city had been too gentle to name, and the gentleness had felt like mercy.

It had not died of anything. The city had culled it, species by species, word by word, and when the work was done, had culled the memory of doing it. Severance was the wages of the killing, and no citizen had ever been shown the bill.

Below the manifest, the rosette opened into the ring.

He swung the arm — the seized collar gave with a sound like a broken seal — and let the sightline drop to the third ring, and the second text stood up out of the stone. Eight blocks, each on its own spoke, each closed with the same two words:

*SUBJECT 01. RETAINED. INTERFACE RECOVERED. TERMINATED.*

He read all eight before he let any of them land, because the taxonomy was the only way he could stand under it. SUBJECT 02. SUBJECT 03. Seven more across the years, each beginning with retained, each ending with terminated.

Between the opening and the closing, the words he could not stop reading: *severance derived from recovered interface; technology turned on citizens.* The city had unbuilt their silence — the same verb the manifest had used for the sky — and fitted it to its own people.

Arthur had set down the severance as the city's invention, a tool like any other. It was not the city's. It was not a tool. It was a murder, and the city had been running on it for twenty years — forty million severed minds, running on one long murder.

The ninth spoke held an entry that was not an entry. Its fields were cut, its designation cut — 09 — and then the stone went flat: *NOT RETAINED. OUTSTANDING.* Nothing else.

Eight blocks ended in TERMINATED, and the ninth ended in nothing, and the nothing had been waiting in the middle of the ring, set down and never closed, for as long as the eight had been dead.

The ring's header named the program in the city's word for it — anomaly — and he read the designation back to himself, then said it into the vault, because it needed to be said in the world:

'The Ninth Anomaly.'

He looked at the cat. The cat was looking at the empty ninth spoke, its ears full of green light, unblinking, and when it spoke there was no mockery in it, only the flat of a long-kept account:

*"You have the numbering. Eight of my kind, Arthur, filed to their deaths. And one that was never retained, because the city could not hold on to it. I have made a very long career of being outstanding."*

'How were the eight found?' he said. 'How were they caught?'

*"They sang,"* the cat said, and the word lay down in the vault and stayed. *"They used what they carried. They spent their interface, and the city followed the sound and took them, one by one. They were the old civilization's last voices, and every one of them came down this corridor singing."*

He understood, before the ring said another word, why the thing on his knees had never once made a sound — and the understanding was so large it took the habit out of him for a moment, the way the green had taken it at the river.

The sound that opened the vault was the same sound the finders followed. Somewhere between the eighth death and this door, the ninth had decided that it would never, not once, spend the only currency it owned. Not for warmth. Not for joy. Not for the sun.

'You have never made a sound,' he said. It was a statement of fact, the way everything he said was a statement of fact, and it cost him more than most.

*"I have been silent since before you were born,"* the cat said. *"I heard the eighth one die with the city's hands on her throat, and I priced what her singing cost her, and I have spent nothing since. You priced it yourself, at the river — a small mortal cat, spending it freely in sun. There is no sun warm enough, Arthur. There has never been warmth in me worth the waste."*

Below the annulus, the rosette ended, and the depth came out of the wall: a well sunk below the floor line, the engraving going down into it, fainter, older-cut, lit by the green sinking to nothing.

He had to lean out of the chair to see it — a long, careful lean, the chair's brake holding.

The third field of the address was not a direction of reading at all. It was a place. And the green gave him, line by line, the shop drawings of his own spine.

He could not have chosen the angle; the well gave it to him. A cross-section, full size, in the old engineer's hand: the citizen restraint — the neural lock — drawn from housing to coil.

Beneath it, the sentence that the whole architecture was one family: binding coil, recovered interface — the interface family of subjects 01 through 08.

The lock on a citizen was the same cage the city had built for the eight, scaled down, repurposed, fitted to the one kind of mind the severance could not reach, so that the one citizen who could remember everything would also be the one who could not move.

Not a treatment. Not a malfunction. A cage — and the cage was the city's care: a twenty-year quarantine of the only mind the cut could not take, built from the technology of the beings the cut had killed.

Below the drawing, in the same careful hand, the release condition — no part number, no procedure, no technician: *only an interface of the family can release the binding.*

And at the foot of the well, in the place where the city kept his own record, stood the word he had read once over a clerk's shoulder and carried ever since as a sentence on himself: *IRREPARABLE.*

In the city's grammar it had meant beyond repair — a verdict, the reason they never tried. In the grammar of the engineers it meant something smaller and crueler: beyond *their* repair.

The city had not stamped him broken. It had stamped him unopenable — a cage they could not pick because the cage was not theirs, and to admit the word was to admit whose it was. He had spent twenty years agreeing with a confession.

'You told me,' he said, level, the way he said everything that had settled — 'that my lock was made of the same kind of thing you are. You did not tell me it was the same thing the eight wore, before me.'

The cat was still, looking at the ninth spoke.

*"I told you the truth at your window,"* it said. *"I did not tell you how many of my kind had worn it before it was fitted to you. I priced your feelings on the way down and spent them carefully — I was getting you here, and the truth has a price, and by the time you could have paid it you would have filed it under 'easier' and stopped carrying me. I am done carrying unsaid things into a vault. The cage is of my kind. The key is of my kind. There is one key in the world, and it is the reason I could not open you at the window — the same wire held both of us, and it had to wait for this room."*

There remained the last page. He had known it was there the way the Archive had known its record ended one page short — an index that promised a final entry, an omission noted as the format's own failure.

The vault had kept the page. It lay in a niche below the well, a narrow slot of failing green, the master copy standing on its spine open to the last plate, waiting for the one reader alive who still owned the grammar.

He read it with the triple he had carried: axis, ring, depth. And the last page told him what the vault was, which was not a warehouse.

*The citizen network terminates here. Every mind the city runs ends in this room; what the city forgets is filed here; what is broadcast here is broadcast everywhere.*

He looked up at the bulkhead, and the hum had a name now: not machinery, not the transit — the net, held, forty million minds breathing in one stone room, and under the green light the wells went down where the eight had been.

It was not a place that stored memory. It was the memory of a city, and it had killed its guardians, and it was humming.

*The carrier key. The memory of life is encoded in the voice-band of the original animal: the purr, twenty to one hundred forty hertz. The carrier must be spent whole — once, at the vault, unamended, unrecorded. Any earlier spending falsifies the key.*

He understood the hoarding then: the silence that had kept the ninth alive was the same silence that kept the key unspent. The voice that would open the vault had to be one that had never sung before — not once in all the suns.

The small mortal cat in the sunlit fragment, purring for no price and no reason, had spent in a single afternoon what the being on his knees had guarded for lifetimes. He had called it the most expensive thing in the fragment. It was the most expensive thing in the world, and it sat on his dead knees, intact.

*The resonance source. The key can open; only a living mind can broadcast. The vault will draw the broadcast through a resonance source: a mind in which the severance did not take — a mind that remembers. The memories are taken one by one, in the order the source gives them, and they do not return.*

He read the last line twice, the way you read a balance twice. A mind in which the severance did not take — the one kind of citizen the cut could not reach, the same kind the city had caged, on the theory that a retainer who could not walk could not carry anything.

The city had spent twenty years building the ideal resonance source and soothing it into immobility. Nothing in the arithmetic had allowed for the one thing that fit both locks: a mind that remembered, carried by a being that had never once spent its one true sound, arriving at the room where the two keys turned together.

He understood the sentence from the corridor at last. It had not been a warning. It had been the terms, read aloud, before he had ever agreed to them — and he had agreed on a windowsill, for a weight on his dead knees and a second of feeling in one toe, and the agreement had been the deal all along.

*When we reach the Seed Vault, you will hate me.*

Not for the lie, and not for the eight, and not for the cage — he could set those down; it was the work of his life.

For the price. The cat had not carried him here to stand. It had carried him here to be spent: the caption and the voice that had counted to ten and the afternoon at thirty-one degrees and the word he had learned to hide, drawn out one by one, in the only order he chose to give them, and they would not return.

And the broadcast would go out across the held breath of the city in the one voice that carried life, and the price of the broadcast was him.

It had been him since the windowsill — the first unsourced warmth, a thing that had crossed the whole city to find the one mind it could spend, and he had opened the box of himself for it without ever being asked.

He looked at the door, still sealed, the one turn of the reader arm that would open it waiting in the collar.

He looked at the thing on his knees that had never made a sound, whose one sound was the key to everything the city had taken, and it looked back at him the way it had looked at the dust word, unblinking, being the first thing in his life that had ever looked at him without wanting him to forget.

And he filed it: the price of the broadcast was him. It had always been him. He had been the price the whole way down — the deal, the terms, the fine print cut into stone — and he could not hate it, because hatred is a memory, and he was the one who would remember.

# Chapter 10: Silence, Then Static

The third watch came down the corridor and found them at the foot of the bulkhead; nothing came with it but the green and the hum. He had been sitting with the price of the broadcast — the door still sealed, the collar still seized, the one turn that would open it waiting in the arm — and the price had not changed.

In the dark the translucency had crept again: the light came through both ears now as through water.

It was the floor that woke them. Not the hum — the floor, then the walls: a deep drumming through the stone, bootfalls in the transit above the dead levels, level after level, a weight coming down.

Then, one by one down the empty levels, the dead loudspeakers came alive — the old overhead horns, wired to the city's amplifiers, rusting in the dark — each coughing a hiss before it spoke, a static he knew the shape of, the static of a city that still owns its wiring. The hiss resolved into a voice, and the voice read the order.

It came by serial, the way everything came: a long alphanumeric, then the shape — *by order of the directorate, the underlevels are sealed, effective immediately. Transit into or out of the dead levels is suspended; every citizen below vertical clearance is to be held and processed under emergency protocol.*

The voice read the cause almost gently, mid-text, the way the city always said the words that killed: a contamination of the dead levels — an anomaly, proven to be in contact with citizens. A registered anomaly contact — the words the gate at the seam had stamped on him, caught up at the foot of the door.

And the last clause came — *the anomaly, and any citizen found in contact with it, to be neutralized at the point of encounter* — and his hand stopped on the chair's arm, and he read it twice, because he had learned to read terrible things twice.

He read it twice, and it did not change.

He thought of the warren — Nadia Soren, who kept only the shape of the word *furry*, and Eli, who would hear every word of the purge and keep none; the words would slide off the child the way they slid off the whole city, in a level about to be swept. He had brought the purge down on the warren. He filed that, and it stayed. Some things would not file.

The cat woke at the change of sound, came up off his knees in a single wound-up motion, one beat slow, ears cocked at the corridor mouth, pupils slits, whiskers pinned flat: the posture of a thing pricing a threat. It looked at the corridor, then at him, and its voice arrived in his head:

*"They have sealed the levels. All of them. The captain has brought the array down to the seam below the ramp — he signed the order at twenty-three fifty-two and has not come down himself. He is running it from above, Arthur. The way he ran your window."*

'The door,' Arthur said, and he was already swinging the arm, the seized collar giving with a sound like a broken seal, the green re-reading the rosette: axis, ring, depth, the spiral paying out under his hand. 'The door is one turn. I know the grammar. I can—'

*"—but the array will not wait for the arm."* The cat's voice was flat, ledger-flat, the voice it used for prices it had already agreed to. *"The lances are the city's instrument for my kind, Arthur. They were built after the eighth, because the city learned that my kind can be burned out of the world by the thing that holds us to it. The coupling — the layer between me and reality.*

*I priced them long ago and have been careful, my whole career, never to stand where they could bring them to bear."*

A pause. Something moved behind the flatness — something he had no name for. *"I have never once stood where they could bring them to bear. Until this corridor."*

The arm came up against the last of its travel — the green standing in the collar, the ring almost read. The bootfalls stopped above them, the worst silence he had ever heard: the silence before.

Then the light changed at the seam, far up the corridor: a whiteness gathering in the lenses of the lance-rings, a white that was not green, and with it a sound — thin, rising, a tone in the band between his implant's hiss and his hearing, with teeth in it — and in the plate of his skull the disconnected hiss began to climb with it, toward a scream —

*"Arthur."* The cat's voice cut through, and there was no mockery in it — none at all — and that, more than the tone and the gathering white, was the most frightening thing it had ever said: *"I am going to hold it."*

'No.' He said it the way he said everything that had settled, level, and it did not settle. 'No. You have priced it. You told me — it burns the coupling. You carried me all this way to be spent at the vault, not here, on a slab, under—'

*"I know what it costs. I priced it before you were born."* And then the thing that was not a price: *"I have never understood why your kind hands over its memory for safety; I priced that question my whole career and never once understood it. I do not understand what I am about to do either — I want to be clear about that.*

*But here is the arithmetic. If I go quiet the way I have stayed quiet for lifetimes, the lances find your signature — small, but a signature — and the burn will take the plate in your skull, and what is in the plate, and what I promised to open. The only way to shield your wire is a shelter built of mine. I am the only thing in this level that can hold it up."*

'Then we go quiet together —' he had the green, the door hanging open in the collar by a finger's width of travel — 'we go through—'

*"The door is one turn from opening and one lifetime from it, and you know it. You know the grammar. You know how long the collar wants. The array is already singing."*

The tone climbed — he felt it in his teeth, in the approved fillings, in the plate of his skull, a wrongness with no name yet — and the cat stood up in the gutter of his knees, forepaws braced on the chair's arm, looking down the corridor into the gathering white, and for one second it was the thing from the windowsill — intact, unspent, mocking the whole city with its stillness.

He would remember that second. He filed it now, so it would survive him: the second it chose.

And then it did not speak. And the world changed.

The shelter came down over the corridor mouth like a tide — stone where the white had gathered, wall where the seam had been, the dead levels reading as empty burned ground, the false world sealing shut over both of them — the last and the largest illusion it had ever made, wider even than the street that had read as wall on the first descent: the same trick, the level read as nothing, only now nothing held it up but the cat, load-bearing, unamended, spending itself deliberately. He saw it do it — the choice in its shoulders, the way it braced, the way a thing braces when it has finished pricing and begun to pay — and he would have reached for it, but the shelter was a held breath, and the light reached the shelter, and found the one thing in all the false stone that was real —

White. A white with no direction, from everywhere at once, through his closed eyes and his clenched hands and the plate of his skull. The sound should have followed it; there was no sound — a silence with weight in it, with depth in it, the wrong silence standing where the roar should have stood, a long held beat. The last thing the coupling held was the noise of the world, and it drank the thunder into itself to buy one more second under the light —

and the coupling let go. He heard it: a soundless snap, the exact sound the hiss had always made when it disconnected, twenty years of that break — and the false world tore from its center, and the real world came back with all its sound at once, and the sound was static: the roar arriving late, the loudspeakers howling, the hiss screaming back into his skull — and the cat was not on the arm of the chair.

It had fallen. It lay at the foot of the chair on the cracked slab, still black, but black the way burned things are black — dim, matte, without the under-light that had once come through ears and tail and the rims of its paws.

The translucency had stopped being edges; the whole of it was dim, the burned parts the color of a screen gone to static, and it lay against the slab like matter that had stopped agreeing to be matter.

He put his hand under its chest — carefully, the way you handle something that has been burned — and it weighed almost nothing. It had been costing itself for a month, in the underlevels, the street, the windowsill before that; what was left was lighter than his memory of it.

It was not dead. He filed that first, the most important filing of his life: not dead.

But the coupling — the layer between it and the world, the thing that had held the shelter and the false walls and the false streets — was burned out of it, and something in its chest moved when it should not, one faint wrong pulse under his hand.

The voice came once, before it failed, scraped thin:

*"—Arthur—"*

and then nothing. And then, much later, in the middle of the screaming static, one more fragment, one word — the word it had learned to write in the dust with its right forepaw, the first matter it had ever made, S-T-A-Y, the A doubled and swept and replaced — pushed through a wire that was burning out:

*"—stay."*

And the link was gone, and the space where the link had been was full of the city's sound.

Searchlights. They had come down the corridor behind the light — troopers at the seam, lenses on their rifles, beams raking the rubble — and found him in the clear: a man in a chair in a circle of white, holding a dim black thing that did not move.

Someone shouted — the sergeant's voice, Torelli's, the man who once lost seven seconds to a wall, calling distances and holds — and above it, through every dead loudspeaker on the level, the captain's voice, short and cold the way his sentences were cold:

'Report. Is it down?'

A pause full of static. A trooper's voice, thin against the roar: 'Field is down, sir. Subject: male. In a chair. Holding the target.'

The loudspeaker said nothing for two full seconds — the kind of nothing that reads a report twice — then Vance's voice, level, administrative, giving him back his life without knowing it:

'Hold.'

'Sir—' Torelli's voice, off.

'Hold.' The same flat word, said twice, the way he read reports twice. Somewhere above the beams the captain was reading an arithmetic he did not yet believe; the beams stayed high, swung lazy, and the order stayed ungiven.

Arthur did not move. His hand was on the cat, flat, feeling the faint wrong pulse of a thing with no right to pulse; the other on the chair's arm.

The arithmetic on his side of the static was small and plain: the array was spent; the sweep was a held gun; the collar waited, one turn engaged, the green standing in the rosette where he had left it; the only direction with a door in it was stone.

The shelter was gone. The ones who would not hold were holding. A door was the only word in the city that still meant *out*. He put his weight on the chair to roll, and the chair died.

The left wheel had sunk through the slab. The discharge had cracked it to friable aggregate, and the wheel had found the crack and turned the crack into a grave; the caster was bound and torqued, the brake cable snapped, the frame grounded on a splinter of stone.

He pressed the right wheel. It spun loose in the air, a dead pedal, useless. The chair that had carried him for twenty years, that the city had soothed him into and called care, refused to move.

He looked at the splinter of stone that had done it, and he filed the whole of it under one word: *cage.*

The cage had outlasted the burn. He had asked it — for the first time in his life — for movement, for the last finger of travel in a collar twenty feet away, and it had given him a spinning wheel and a snapped cable.

He sat in the ruins of it, holding the crippled cat. Nothing held him now, nothing carried him. The man who had not walked in twenty years looked at the door that was one turn from opening and did the only thing left that was not in the city's books.

He gathered the cat. Not by the scruff — carefully, the right hand under the chest, the left cradling the burned dim weight of it, the way you carry something spent, close, against the sternum — and it settled against him, lighter than his memory of it; the empty link where its voice had been pressed close, like a held note.

Then he put both hands on the arms of the chair — the cage that had carried him, that had failed him — and pushed, and the chair let him down to the ground.

He came down hard. The dead legs came with him, cargo — he did not feel the landing, never felt the legs — and he was on the floor of the corridor, on the cracked slab, under the searchlights, holding the thing that could no longer move, ten feet of clear rubble between him and the door.

The man who had not walked in twenty years put his weight on one forearm and hauled, dragging the dead weight of his legs behind him like a second body, the cat held against his chest with the other arm, and began, with his arms, to cover the distance.

He could not walk. The city had made certain of that — had built the cage into his spine, had fit the lock into the one mind the severance could not reach and called the stillness care. But the city had never once, in all its arithmetic, priced a man who was willing to crawl.

He moved himself, on his arms. The one thing worth carrying had been placed in his arms, and there was no one left to carry him.

The slab was cold through his palms — the first floor he had felt in twenty years. He braced, hauled, dragged; the dead legs scraped and did not hurt, and the not-hurting was the city's gift and he used it.

The cat lay against his sternum, dim and still, its one word spent, its silence intact, its voice — the key, the unspent key, the only thing the city had never been able to take — pressed warm and whole between them.

Behind him the loudspeakers said the word back to the level — *hold* — and ahead, the green light stood in the collar he had turned, waiting for the rest of the turn, and the beams swept wide and lazy.

Ten feet became nine, and nine became eight, and the man who could not walk, carrying the cat that could no longer move, with the whole of the false arithmetic and the true behind him, began to crawl.

# Chapter 11: The Offer

One set of boots. He heard them before the beams moved, before anything in the corridor changed — a single tread, heel first, unhurried, coming down from the seam, and behind it the sound of a sweep going quiet, the way a held gun goes quiet. Arthur stopped mid-pull, one hand flat on the cracked slab, the cat against his sternum, and did not move. A month of running had taught him the difference between boots that find you and boots that have already found you. These had already found him.

The tread stopped two arm's lengths from his hands. The searchlight that had been raking the rubble swung wide and stayed, holding the far wall, and a man walked into the reach of the beam — polished boots, a uniform bearing captain's bars, no helmet, no haste — and stood between Arthur and the door, the green of the collar burning at his back like a small sun, his shadow thrown across the slab toward the crawling man.

Arthur filed him by what could be seen, by the visible surfaces: Enforcement, captain's rank, forty, the flat administrative posture of a man whose days pass through his hands and do not stick to them. He had never filed one who had already filed him. Then the man spoke, and the voice resolved the file the way a signature resolves a document.

'Arthur.'

He knew the voice. It had said *hold* twice from the dead loudspeakers, and the beams had held, and the sweep had held, and the order had stood unserved between one word and the next. It was the captain's voice — the man the cat had named in its last full sentence, *he is running it from above, the way he ran your window* — and it was saying his name now, in the air between the two of them, with no loudspeaker between and no static over it.

'You know me,' Arthur said. It came out level. Twenty years of the city had worn surprise out of him to a small, dry item; a man knowing his name was smaller than the things he had stopped being surprised by.

'I read your file. Twice.'

'Then you know I'm not supposed to be here.'

'You're not supposed to be alive,' Vance said. 'The order says neutralize at the point of encounter. I'm holding the order.'

Arthur heard the word before the sense: *holding* — the same word, held a third time, now in a man's mouth instead of a speaker's. He set it beside the two holds that had come before it, and the column meant nothing yet, and he left it standing, because some columns are not for reading. They are for watching grow.

'One,' Vance said. 'The order has your name on it. Two. I am the one who holds it. Three. I can hold it long enough to say what I came down to say.' He paused, the way a man pauses who has already priced what comes next. 'It isn't the order I came down to serve. It's the offer.'

The corridor became a room. Far back at the seam a trooper shifted, and the sound did not carry; there were the two of them, and the dim black thing between the crawling man's arm and his chest, and the green, and nothing else that mattered. Vance gave the terms the way he gave everything: short sentences, in order, without amendment.

'The city offers you the following, through me. You surrender the anomaly. You submit to repair and cleansing.'

'The record is amended — no contact, no fugitive, no level; a clerk who was away, and reconciled. The flat is yours. The archive is yours.'

'The cage that locked you is the same procedure that can stand you; the city built it, and the city keeps the key, and the key turns the other way. You walk out of the level, Arthur. You walk up the ramp, out of the dead levels, into the standing world.'

'And none of it will hurt. No memory of any pain. Those are the terms.'

Arthur was quiet a long time. Vance did not rush him; a man who has priced a thing correctly can afford to wait, and Arthur put that at the back of the column too, where the unreadable things accumulate: the captain had priced him.

And the price was fair. He felt the offer the way he had felt the one second of feeling in his dead toe — as a deposit on standing — and it was enormous, and his weighing of it was honest, because he had spent twenty years hoarding the word *stand* in the lowered drawer where he kept the things he did not let out.

The flat. The sill, clean of the shape that had once presided over it, the way sills had been clean before. The archive, its dust, its forbidden rows, his rows, the files that only he could read and only he could bury.

Standing — the whole city rearranged to the height of a man: lights at chest level, counters, door handles, the faces of the standing brought down to his eye level by a world that no longer held him at its floor. His mother's counting would cool in its drawer, and the somewhere else it promised would stop hurting, and the counting would become a fact like any fact, a number, eleven, a piece of furniture. The page would lie flat in its grave, the tabby on the windowsill, the caption he had learned to keep warm gone cold, and it would be a page, only a page, the way pages are for everyone else, and the word behind his teeth, the first word, the one the whole city had lost, would stop glowing.

All of it: the afternoon at thirty-one degrees, the last afternoon he had stood in the standing world; the pain of twenty years, itemized and removed; and the legs, and the life, and the peace. It was the city's arithmetic, and it was the first arithmetic the city had ever offered that closed. The column balanced. It balanced, and the balance was the most frightening thing the city had ever shown him, because his fear had never been that it would hurt to remember. His fear had been that none of it mattered. The offer did not take the fear away. The offer confirmed the fear, and removed the mattering.

And then the archivist in him — the one thing the city had never been able to sever — did what it always did on the receipt of any document: checked the terms against the inventory. The offer itemized the legs and the flat and the amendment. It itemized the pain, to be removed. It set a single price — the anomaly, surrendered — and the price was honest, because the anomaly could no longer speak to him or hold a shelter over him; it was a dim weight, lighter than his memory of it, a faint wrong pulse under his thumb. Everything was itemized. Everything was priced. There was only one error in the document, and it was the hinge: the inventory was not pain.

'The terms,' Arthur said, 'have no serial.'

Vance did not move. 'The offer is not entered.'

'Everything the city does is entered,' Arthur said. 'Every order has a number. Every lock has a form. Every cage has a file. You came down alone, with an unnumbered procedure, to a marked man under a standing order. You held the sweep. You held the order. Now you're holding out your own hand, and the hand isn't in any book.' He looked up the standing height of the man, the green at his back, and asked it flat: 'Whose offer is this?'

'It is the city's offer,' Vance said. 'I am the hand that delivers it.'

'Then where's the serial?' Arthur said. 'The city signs everything, captain. Even the things it declines to do. An unnumbered offer is a rumor wearing rank.'

Vance was silent — not the smooth silence of the severed, the other silence, the one that reads a document twice and finds something the first reading missed. When he spoke, he spoke the way a man reads a line he has read many times, without weight.

'You were eight. Nineteen ninety-eight. The raid on the flat. One picture book, confiscated, graded high. The child behind the door, reciting the page, over and over, while my men took the book — a boy who would not stop saying what he saw. I remember the voice. The grid has never been able to amend that out of me.' A beat, measured. 'I led that raid, Arthur. I signed the order that locked you.'

The words arrived, and Arthur filed them the way he had filed the rosette: whole. Year, 1998. Subject: minor, age eight. Grounds: retention, irreparable. Scope: permanent, irreversible. Signature: the hand that now stood over him in the light, offering to turn the key the other way.

He checked the three things a clerk checks on any document: the date, which fit; the scope, which he had carried for twenty years in his own spine; the hand, which was in front of him, within reach, hanging empty at the man's side. All three reconciled. He had sat, he had lain, he had crawled under a piece of paper for twenty years, and the paper had had no face, and the face was the city's, and now the paper had a face, and the face was speaking to him reasonably, at the level of his crawling, because it had read his file twice.

He did not shout. He put that with the rest of it: he had never once, in twenty years, pictured the signer. The order had been a signature without a man. It had been easier to carry, that way. Now there was a man, and Arthur was sorry to lose the facelessness — in a precise and useless way, the way you are sorry to lose a document that told you less than the truth.

'Then you know the stamp,' Arthur said. 'IRREPARABLE. You know the city's procedure never worked on me. The only form it ever took was the lock, and the lock never cured anything. What are you offering me that you know won't take?'

'It has never been run on a willing subject,' Vance said. 'It takes when the mind agrees to it.' He said it flatly, and under the flatness Arthur heard the man not believing what he was delivering — the amendment considered, and refused, and delivered anyway. 'The city's record says consent lowers the failure rate. I read it twice. I did not believe it either time. I am delivering it regardless, because the offer is the offer, and my belief is not a term of it.'

'You believe in paper,' Arthur said. 'Your kind of paper — the kind that can't be amended. You're standing here holding an order you won't serve, delivering a cure you don't believe in, and the only document in this corridor that is true is the one you signed twenty years ago.'

'It is the truest document I have ever signed,' Vance said.

'You remember,' Arthur said. It was not a question. 'The voice. The page. The boy behind the door. All of it, in a city where nobody keeps anything. You're the second thing in the world I've ever met that I can't price. The first one is in my arms.'

Vance looked at the cat — the first time, Arthur thought, the man had ever stood close enough to do it — and the look was long, and Arthur could not read it, and he left it unread, the third unreadable entry of his life, after the face pressed against his hand at the gate of signatures. The cat did not stir. The faint wrong pulse went on under Arthur's thumb, and the empty link where its voice had been gave back nothing.

'Answer me, then,' Vance said. 'You're the only man in the city who can answer honestly. Twenty years in a cage for one page of a book. A month of running for a thing that cannot even reach you anymore — cannot shelter you, cannot speak to you, cannot price its own way out. It has one foot in static and the other in the dark, Arthur. Was it worth it?'

And Arthur, who could not lie, set the question down in front of himself and looked at it the way he had looked at the offer: honestly. The honest answer rose up whole. It had been true since the raid, and it had been sleeping in the lowered drawer with the word *stand*, and the offer had woken it: he did not know. Of course he did not know — the offer was the city's proof of it, the whole force of the offer was that he did not know — and he let it stand, and did not run from it, and said it.

'I don't know.'

Vance waited. When Arthur gave him nothing more, he said: 'There. That is the answer the city exists to remove. You just told me yourself.'

'No,' Arthur said. 'I told you I don't know. That isn't the pain, captain. That's the why. You took the legs — you took them yourself, with your pen, the day I was eight — and you left me the why. It's the only clause of your arithmetic I ever agreed to. When you took my legs, at least you let me keep the why.'

'And does the why stand you up?' Vance said. 'Does it feed you? Does it amend your file? It has cost you your legs, your flat, your life, and the thing in your arms, and it still cannot tell you whether it was worth it.'

'It's all I've got that's true,' Arthur said. 'You said it yourself — you remember the voice. You remember the page. That's what the city took from everyone else and couldn't take from us. You made your peace with that, captain. You've been signing ever since, to keep the arithmetic closed. I haven't signed anything. Take the legs out of your offer and what's left is a sale of the why, and I'm not selling. I'm not signing a second order.'

He put his hand flat on the slab and pulled.

The sidearm came up when he pulled. He heard it clear the holster — a sound he had heard before in his life, from the door of his flat, from the corridor of his flat's night, from the mouth of the seam — and he did not stop. The man who had not walked in twenty years pulled, and dragged the dead weight of his legs behind him like a second body, the cat riding in the cradle of his arm, one reach, two, the green of the collar ahead of him like the far end of a calculation — and the shot did not come. He could feel the barrel on his back the way you feel a glance, a pressure with a direction, following him for a reach and a half — and then the sound of the weapon being lowered, holstered, settled: the shot unspent, the order unserved, and nothing in the corridor to say why, except the man himself, who said nothing.

He did not look back. He had learned, in a month of running, not to look at the thing that had not killed him; looking made it real, and real was a category he was running out of room for.

He reached the ring of green light at the collar, the door still sealed, the grammar of the turn waiting under his hand — the archived page had told him the vault kept three doors: codes, then signatures, and then a third, which the page had not named, the way documents stop where the deep begins — and he had begun the turn when the captain spoke from behind him, measured, unhurried, once, the way a man says a thing he has decided to say exactly once. And this time there was no number on it. No *one, two, three*. Just a sentence, set down on the slab at his back like a paper:

'The inner door wants a clean signature. Yours is dirty.'

# Chapter 12: The Ledger, 1998

He came back up the ramp at 23:10, and the men at the seam did not ask.

That was the thing about the men who had served under him longest. Torelli had taught them, or they had taught each other: there were questions that cost more than the silence they covered. The EMP array stood where it had been staged, in the cold at the bottom of the ramp, and the trooper beside it came to attention and did not speak, and Vance walked past him, through the gate, up into the standing world, and the net closed behind him without comment.

The net was quiet. That was the first fact, and he did not write it down, because there was nothing about the net's quiet worth recording. The sweep had found nothing. The flat's watchers had reported nothing, once a day, as always. The sealed levels had been amended out of the city's own memory during the night, the way everything amended, and the boy was down there somewhere with the dim weight of the anomaly in his arms, and Vance had let him go, and the order still stood — unserved, in his name, unsigned by any outcome, like a signature no one had countersigned.

He did not go to the desk. That was the second fact, and he noted it without approving it: he walked past the terminal with its even green, past the chair, to the shelf — a block of city-forged board bolted to the wall, and on it the first volume, between the binders the building no longer opened.

The spine had gone soft. It had been soft the last two times he had put his hand on it — a month ago, two nights ago — and both times he had taken the hand back. The book did not ask anything. That was the thing about paper: it waited, and it did not care how long. The shelf had never once reported him.

He took it down.

It was lighter than the grid's records, heavier than its own pages, and it opened without a sound. The paper had yellowed the way paper yellowed before the city stopped making it — a color that had no formula, because the color was time, and time was one of the things the city amended. The ink was his.

He knew the hand before he read a word of it. The V, open like a door. The letters carried over the line — a weight he had agreed to carry. The hand he had had at twenty, in the summer of 1998, at thirty-one degrees, in a flat whose window had been open without permission, the heat coming in off a street the city no longer admitted it had ever had. He turned the leaves past the early entries — the yard duty, the first requisition, the first signature he had been trusted to make whole — and came to the long entry of the raid, and read the page all the way through, for the first time in twenty years.

> 1-0409. Time 17:40. July, 1998. The flag: household, surplus account, stock high. Detail: four men, myself, light arms, no force required. Item: one picture book, graded high, grade his, stock destined for destruction.
> The heat today is unpermitted. Thirty-one at the walk; the window in the main room is open without authorization. The room has no official temperature. I record the fact because it is the kind of fact the grid will smooth, and someone should know the room was hot.
> The book is light in the gloved hand. I record that it was heavier than it had been in its owner's keeping. I do not know why that is true, and I record it because it is true.
> The child stayed behind a door that stayed closed. He did not come out when called. He recited — from behind the panels, while the book was in transit, four times, and did not stop when we stopped listening — a page of a child's rhyme about a cat. He could not have been reading it. He could not see the book. I wrote the page down as he said it, line by line, because the grid will not keep it and something should. It reads:
>
> > Tabby on the windowsill, the sun across her fur.
> > She keeps it all the morning long, and keeps it close to her.
> > The sill is wide, the day is long, and no one has to know it —
> > A cat keeps the sun the way a child keeps a secret.
>
> The men stood with their backs to it, the way men stand with their backs to what they have no orders about. I held the book and did not turn my back.
> The matter is reconciled. Record by my own hand only.

He read the page all the way through. That was the third fact, and he set it down carefully, the way a man sets down a weight he has been carrying for twenty years and only now learns the number of: *he had never read this page all the way through.* He had kept the volume on the shelf. He had kept it against his hand twice in a month. He had known what the middle of it contained — and he had never once read it to the end, because the end was where the young hand had written the page down, and the page was the one thing in the city the grid could not take back from him.

The anomaly could not enter his mind. His firewall was military grade; it had held against the anomaly in the dark of the sealed levels, and he had stood in the pressure of it and the pressure had slid off. This did not enter through the grid. There was no amendment in it, no slide. It walked in off a page, in ink he had put there himself, at twenty, in the heat.

One. The grid amends.
Two. Minds take the cut.
Three. Paper holds.
Four. Paper holds even what a man writes down so that he will never have to read it.

He read the rhyme a second time.

*Tabby on the windowsill, the sun across her fur.* The boy was behind the door. The book was in transit in the room. The men stood with their backs to the voice. The child recited the page whole — four times — and he could not have been reading it, because he could not see it, and he could not have been old enough to have been told it more than once. He had it, whole, in a city where nothing stayed. He had it *before* the raid. The raid could not take it out of him. In two seasons the book would burn with the rest of the grade-high stock, the page with it; the page survived in two places only — the child's memory, which the city would spend the next twenty years trying to make stop meaning anything, and this book.

The file had said the voice was not in any record — *the boy's voice behind the door, which the record did not contain* — and for twenty years he had believed it, and never checked it, because checking would have meant opening the volume, and opening the volume would have meant reading the page. The file was right, as far as it went: no record the city kept had contained the voice. The record had been him. He had written it down the night it happened, so that it would not be lost, and locked it in a drawer and carried it for twenty years without opening it: the wall where the map had kept a passage.

There was a difference between keeping a page and reading a page. He had done the one for twenty years. He had just done the other — and the other had entered him the way nothing had entered him since he was twenty.

The city called it a defect. He turned past the raid entry to the next, the shorter one, and read it whole, and it was all there, in the young hand's even lines: the retention test, run after the confiscation, which had proved what the raid had already shown — the child had kept the page after it had left his keeping. The memory did not slide. It did not amend. It stayed, whole, in a city that ran on things not staying. That was the defect. That was the grounds. *IRREPARABLE* — the stamp for the work the city did not intend to finish, and had never amended in twenty years. And on the order, in the delegation line, in the V open like a door, his name.

> 1-0410. Time 21:05. August, 1998. Item: the lock order, signed by my hand today. Subject: the child of 1-0409. Scope: permanent, irreversible.
> The test confirmed the grade. He held the page after the confiscation; the instruments measured it; the retention is genuine. The city will use him in the archive, the way the sighted are used, to bury what they see.
> The book is in the grade-high stock. It will burn. I have the page in this volume and he has it in his head, and both of us signed today. I do not know which of the two the city will find it harder to amend.

The boy's voice did not need a door, then or now. It had gone through the panels that night and into the hand of a twenty-year-old who had thought he was taking custody of a book. He had been taking custody of a page.

He turned the leaf. The volume ended two entries after that. He had known the last page was coming; he had known it for twenty years, the way you know the number that is going to come up; the knowing was the shape the memory had left behind it. He turned to it and read the last entry of the first volume, in the hand of the man he had been:

> 1-0411. Time 02:10. Volume one closed.
> The city has its record and I have mine. The child has the page in him and I have it in this book, and between us it will survive, and we have both signed.
> Today I was certain I did the right thing.

The rest of the page was blank. Twenty years of blank page after the certainty.

He sat with it. The building made its permitted noises and none of its real ones. The room was at its permitted temperature. Below, in the cold the city had sealed and forgotten how to count, a boy — a man now, twenty years of lock, twenty years of archive dust — was crawling toward a door that wanted a clean signature, and his was dirty, and there was exactly one clean signature left in the city, and Vance had spent the evening reading the only page that could make him use it as he was going to.

He opened the drawer and took out book three — the current volume — and wrote, because writing was the discipline, and the discipline was the only thing that had ever held:

> 3-0716. Time 23:41. Re-read volume one in full. First complete reading. Item: the raid entry, the page, the closing entry, the certainty line. The grid does not know of this reading. This page is the record of it.
> I carried the page for twenty years without reading it. Tonight I read it three times.
> Decide what it means that the boy could not stop saying it, and that I could not stop writing it down.

He closed book three and returned it to the drawer. Then he turned back to the first volume, to the last page, to the blank space beneath the young hand's certainty, and he wrote there — not an entry, no number, nothing the grid would call a record, no date, nothing but the words, in the same hand, the V open the same way, the ink the same color by the light of the same building:

> Today I read it three times. I did not find the certainty.

The pen made the line. The pressure stayed in the fiber, the way he had told Torelli it would, years ago: the pressure is a witness. He looked at the two lines for a long time — the certainty at twenty, its absence at forty, one page, one hand, the same door — and then he closed the volume, and he did not put it back on the shelf.

He put it inside his coat, against his ribs.

The drawer was still open. The pen was dry. The ink waited — and under the pen, in the same drawer, the thing he had not touched since the requisition had gone through: the officer's signature key.

It was a wafer of fired ceramic, thumb-sized, sleeved in the black alloy of his command, and there was no printing on it anywhere, no number, no stamp — the city's hand had never crossed it. That was the point. It carried his signature: not a copy of it, the signature itself, cut into the ceramic in the year he had been commissioned, before the grid had learned to re-key a man's identity in its own hand.

Pressed to a reader, the wafer presented him as he had been signed — clean, unmarked, military grade — the one identity in the city that had never been flagged, because it was the only identity the city had never had to amend.

The deepest gates wanted more than the wafer's word. They wanted the wafer *and* the man, alive, holding it. That was the nature of a signature: it was only a signature while the hand that made it lived. His hand had lived for twenty years. It had written tonight, in the first volume, beneath the certainty.

He took the key out of the billet. It was warm from the drawer, warmer than the room, and he did not weigh it in his palm. He put it in the pocket over his heart.

He stood. The volume lay against his ribs; the key lay over his heart; the sidearm hung at his hip, where it had hung when it had not fired in the corridor. His hand did not go to it. That was the fourth fact, and he let it stand unnumbered in the record of the night: the hand that had not fired went instead to the coat, where the key was, and held it through the cloth, and he walked out.

Torelli was at the desk at the end of the hall, working the late close-out. Vance did not ask him to log anything, and Torelli did not ask what a captain was carrying toward the ramp at that hour. He said only: 'Sir.' One word, low, in his measured voice — and no more; the silence after it had the weight of a measured floor, and it held. The building let him out. The gate let him through. The night had no birds in it — the night's whole history in one word — and he did not look up at it.

The direction of his boots was down.

He was going to the Seed Vault, where the boy was crawling toward the inner door, the one that wanted a clean signature. Three doors stood between them and the thing the vault was built to forget how to be: codes, signatures, and the last one, which had a category for exactly one kind of hand. Vance's was the only one left in the city that was clean. He had signed against the boy at twenty, in the heat, in the hand that was writing now beneath his heart. Tonight he was going down to sign *for* him.

One. The book is under my coat.
Two. The key is over my heart.
Three. The order is in my name, unserved, and I am carrying it to the one door that has a category for my name.
Four. Let it open.
Five. The direction is down. The direction is the Seed Vault.

# Chapter 13: Three Doors

The words followed him all the way to the collar — *the inner door wants a clean signature. Yours is dirty.* The sentence had been standing behind him since the captain spoke it, and Arthur put it in the drawer with the things he could not use yet, and took hold of the collar. The metal was warm from his own hands, warm the way iron gets when a man has been gripping it half the night. Half-advanced, the exact shape his grip had left in it on the night the searchlights found them — the first half of the turn had cost him a wheelchair and a night and everything a wheelchair had ever meant, and the second half stood ready, waiting to be finished.

His arms had been shaking since the ramp; the crawl had taken the night's worst out of them, and the collar asked for more than the night had left. He gave it what he had — his whole technique for a month: give what you have, pull, do not stop pulling. The green ring of light lay low around the collar, and the cat lay against his sternum, and the city lay somewhere above them both, amended, quiet.

'Stay,' he said to the cat. Out of habit. It could not have moved if the ceiling came down; it could not have heard him if it could. The dim weight in the cradle of his arm did not stir, and the link where its voice had lived gave back nothing, and he knew the word was for him now, not for it — but it was the word it had left him with, and the word was his to keep.

The page was where he had left it, eight years deep, whole, exact: the engineer's clearance, memorized because the city paid its retainers to remember and then paid them again to keep quiet. The page said the vault kept three doors, and it said the outer door was not sealed against intrusion. It was sealed against ignorance — and the engineers had never revoked their clearance, because the city had forgotten the door was open to anyone who remembered it.

AX-7. R-3. D-24. The axis first: he turned, and the detents came up under his fingers like the teeth of a key — one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, seated — and the collar settled a degree deeper into the throat of the vault.

The ring ran the other way, finer, three detents. He counted by touch, because the green light stood behind him and he had long since learned to trust his fingertips over the city's glow. Three. Seated. A weight came off the mechanism, the way a held breath comes off a room.

Depth, last. The page had said seat to twenty-four, and the engineers had scratched their own index into the steel where no amendment could reach it — a notch no grid had ever owned. He lined the scratch to the collar's witness and pressed. The thread took. There was a silence that belonged entirely to the door, and then the door unsealed with a long groan, as if it were remembering the last time it had been asked to move, and the green beyond it stood in the corridor like a column of still water.

The seal had been built to hold for a hundred years against men with equipment and orders. It had not been built against a man who remembered, because the city had forgotten that remembering was itself a tool. The door stood open on the clearance of a dead engineer and the hands of a clerk they had spent twenty years trying to empty. Arthur carried the fact with him, because it was the first door of his life that had opened for him instead of at him.

The cat had weighed more when it was mocking him. He remembered that weight the way you remember a page you have not opened, and he did not open it now. He went through — one hand flat on the wall, the cat in the other arm — into the first corridor of the deep. Three doors, the page had said: codes, then signatures, and a third it had not named, the way documents stop where the deep begins. The first stood open behind him now, opened on knowledge. It had not asked who he was. It had asked whether he knew, and he knew.

The corridor ran blind for six meters — smooth wall, no doors, no seams — and ended at a second door, smaller, powered, awake. The air was colder here, and the floor had been wiped that same morning by machines that had never stopped believing the visitors would come. A plate sat low in the door, at the height of a kneeling man's hand. The vault had been built for standing men; the plate had been set for them, and Arthur reached it from the floor, and that had been true of every door in his life.

Arthur knew this gate. He had passed through its cousin at the seam — the brand-new gate of signatures — and the passing had cost him nothing that night and everything by the following morning: the reclassification, the registered contact, the flight that had ended here, at the second door of the deep, with the same mark they had stamped on him travelling with him the whole way. He pressed his palm to the glass, and the glass was warm.

The plate read him the way the seam gate had read him: slowly, whole, unhurried — a signature examined rather than a man. The light came up amber. VALID. And beneath it, without judgement, as a fact among facts: MARKED. Filed, he thought, where the city filed him.

'Marked, but valid,' Arthur said aloud — the phrase he had used at the seam, the one that had been true then and was true still, the exact sentence a man says to a door he has been through before — and the door opened on it with a click that was almost kind, and for half a breath he let himself believe the third door would be kind too.

The second door had asked exactly one question: were you yourself? Not his file. Not his history. Not the weather of the life behind him — the man, alive, present, continuous, the signature held in the body, where it could not be edited. The mark rode on him like weather, and the self underneath it was still his, and the door had let the two of them through together: counted, flagged, admitted in the same breath. Honesty from the city was so rare he carried it a step past the threshold.

He knew, even then, that it would not be. But what he carried through had no row in the gate's book: the door had a category for him and none for the dim weight in his arms, and it did not ask, and it let the unreadable thing through on his signature, the way every door in the city had, and in that much the city had never once been wrong about him.

One corridor more, shorter, colder. The last door stood at the end of it, and it was not a collar and not a gate: a plate of pale metal, seamless, set at the height of his heart when he knelt — the vault's third seal, the one the page had not named. No handle. No mechanism. The first door had asked whether he knew. The second had asked who he was. This one, he understood now, would ask what had been done to him.

He had known what it would want. Vance had told him: a warning, then a description, then, in the end, a sentence he could not use. He pressed his palm to the plate anyway. A signature that does not offer itself is not a signature, and a man who has come as far as this does not stop at the last door to spare himself the refusal.

The plate read him slowly. Cooler than the middle gate. This one was not checking whether he existed — it was checking what had happened to him. Light ran under the glass, and the line of his living signature took shape, in green: pulse and warmth and the particular bioelectric habit of a body that had kept what it was told to forget, twenty years of it, held whole in a city that ran on things not staying. Then the green went out. The red came up, and it was the red of the archive's own stamps, and Arthur read his life on the last door of the deep in four lines:

> SIGNATURE: MARKED
> STATUS: IRREPARABLE
> CLEAN SIGNATURE REQUIRED
> ACCESS DENIED

The word was his. It had been his since he was eight, chosen for him on an afternoon at thirty-one degrees, while a door stood shut between a child and the room where they were taking his page. He had read it on paper a hundred times, on the one file of him that counted. He had never seen it printed on the world itself, on the last door of the deep, in red, turning him away. And that was the whole shape of his life in a single act: the city let him see, and let him carry, and let him arrive, and the arrival was the refusal. The sighted are used to bury what they see. They are not used to being let past the burial.

He pressed the plate again, and the door read him again, and there it was again — the same four lines, the same word, patient as a verdict. He offered it his other hand, as if the mark might live in the right one and not the left, and the door read the left the same way, whole, and said the same four lines. The cat was a weight in his arm, wrong-light, wrong-soft. The empty socket where its voice had been gave back nothing. 'Marked. Irreparable,' he said aloud, so that at least one of them heard it whole — and the corridor said nothing, and the door said its four lines, and there was nothing in the reach of his voice or his hands that would open it.

And somewhere in the middle of that Arthur understood what the city had built down here, behind three doors: a room that would admit the unmarked and no one else — its own clean citizens, the ones it had never had to touch — while the one man in the city who had kept the old life in his head carried the thing to the threshold and was refused at the threshold itself. The body that remembers cannot pass. The last lock was not in his spine. It was in the door, and the door was the city's, and it held.

He had opened every lock in his life by finding its document — a schedule, a code, a forgotten clearance, some page that said more than it was supposed to say. This door had no document. There was no seam to it, no register, no page anywhere that covered a clean signature, because the deep was where documents stopped. Even the collar had been a page. This was the first thing in his life that had never been a document — just a door, and it wanted what he did not have.

He gathered the cat closer, the way you gather a thing before setting it down for good. 'We got as far as the door,' he told it. Not a question. It was not a question anything could answer. The dead link gave back nothing, and he was still meaning the words, still holding them between himself and the dim weight, when the boots came.

Boots.

He heard them through the corridor, through the door, through his own silence. One tread, heel first, unhurried — the tread that had come down the ramp on the night of the offer, the tread of a man who has priced what comes next. The middle gate admitted him without a pause: one clean read, the click of a signature that had never once been marked, and then the boots came on through the second door and into the corridor, and the man walked into the chamber the way a man walks into a room he has already decided to be in.

He had come through the outer door on Arthur's knowledge — left standing open, the first thing the archived clearance had ever let through besides Arthur himself — and through the middle gate on a read that had not cost him a breath. And the coat at his chest stood out a hand's width: a squared weight, the exact shape of a bound volume, carried against the ribs the way other men carried their orders. Arthur read the shape. Paper. The captain had come down to the deepest door in the city carrying paper, and the city ran on data, and paper was the one thing the city could not amend.

He did not look at Arthur. He did look at the plate — Arthur saw the eyes pause on the four red lines, the way a man reads his own name in a document he has been carrying toward — and then he put his hand inside his coat and brought out the wafer.

Thumb-sized. Fired ceramic, sleeved in black alloy, no printing, no number. The officer's signature key — Arthur knew its class from the archive, an identity the grid had never had to amend, the only identity the city had never touched, because it predated the touching. It was warm from the captain's coat, warmer than the chamber, warmer than anything in the deep had a right to be — the warmth of a thing kept over the heart. Arthur understood in the same instant what the third door had been built to admit: one kind of hand. Clean. Military. Unmarked. The hand that had signed his lock order.

'It reads me the way you said it would,' Arthur said.

Vance did not answer with words. He brought the wafer down to the plate and bent over it, the way a man bends to a document he means to sign, and pressed, and set his hand over it and held — the ceramic and the living man, the signature and the hand that had made it — because a signature is only a signature while the hand that made it lives, and this hand had lived twenty years past what it had signed. The plate read him the way it had read Arthur, cool and thorough, and this time the green did not fail.

The red went out first. Then the four lines went down, one by one, as if the door were un-reciting them, and the green came up whole, and the door unsealed with the same long groan as the first, and Vance said it without looking at him, the way a man signs without looking at the pen:

'Twenty years ago I signed a form. This one today — same handwriting.'

There was no fencing in it, no amendment, no offer. A fact, laid down the way he laid down his facts — and then the standing man stepped back from the door he had opened, and stood aside, and offered the threshold to the crawling man with no word at all. Arthur had no answer that would not have been smaller than the fact, so he gave none.

He went through, and the door stood open behind him on the same handwriting that had closed a different door twenty years ago. Some things are carried, not filed — and he carried it, past the offered threshold, past the man who had signed for him, the cat riding in the cradle of his arm, into the deepest room of the vault.

The deepest room was not a server room.

No racks. No glass. No cabinets of the net, no consoles, nothing the city would have called its heart. Under the floor, somewhere Arthur could not name, a machine went on breathing — the low, constant hum of a thing that had been running since before the afternoon at thirty-one degrees, and would not have stopped if the city had fallen on it. He felt it in the floor of his palms. The chamber was round and cold, lit by the same green at half strength, and in it stood nine pods.

Eight were empty. They stood in a ring, like teeth in a jaw — man-length, ceramic, the seams of their hatches sealed and dusted fine under the green. Cultivation pods, built to grow a kind of life the city had stopped admitting existed. Along their inner walls ran the residue of a nutrient lattice, a fine sediment of something grown and gone. Arthur had read the files of the eight; here was where the files had been kept, and the files had gone, and the keepers with them. What had grown in them had grown and gone; the pods kept the shape of it, the way a bowl keeps the shape of what it held. Eight of them, and the ninth.

The ninth stood apart, at the center of the ring, where a prisoner would never have been put. Its hatch was swung back — not broken, not forced; swung, the way a door is swung when nothing was ever meant to hold it shut. Where the eight had seals, the ninth had a throat: a bore, an emitter lattice, the careful architecture of a broadcasting instrument, aimed up through the ceiling of the deep, at the city above it, at everything the net had ever carried. No sediment lined its wall. Nothing had ever been grown in it, because it had never been a place for growing. The plaque had called it outstanding, never retained — and this was why. It had been waiting to send.

Under Arthur's thumb the faint wrong pulse beat once — not a sound, nothing the room could hear, only the thin pressure of the thing in his arms answering, in the only register it had left.

It had never been a prison.

It was a transmitter.

# Chapter 14: The Vault

Arthur knelt at the centre of the ring, under the open throat of the ninth pod, one hand flat on the floor, the cat in the curve of his arm. The floor was warm in a way the cold green chamber had no right to be. Under his palm something breathed — slow, whole, older than the city that sat on top of it — the same machine he had felt on the way in, running since before the afternoon at thirty-one degrees. And the stone was not stone. It gave the barest way under his weight, the way living matter gives, and the hum that filled the chamber was not a machine's hum. It was a body's.

Vance stood where the door had let him through, at the edge of the green, saying nothing. The paper volume rode against his ribs. He had carried it down three doors and through the deepest one, and now he stood like a man holding a place for a document he had not opened — present, closed, waiting to be read. Arthur left him there, unread himself.

His own body's accounts were all in arrears. The arms that had hauled him down the ramp and through the collar were down to a long, deep tremor; his knees, numb in the old way, had begun to register the floor's warmth as something almost like feeling. He was kneeling at the centre of the deepest room in the city, in borrowed balance, holding the only thing in the world that weighed nothing and everything, and he did not know how long he could hold either.

Then the dead socket gave back a word.

It came the way it had come on the night of the EMP — thin, wrong-edged, a voice heard under water: *Arthur.* Not sound. Nothing the room could hear. The link where its voice had lived lit for one syllable and guttered, and under Arthur's thumb the wrong thin pulse gathered itself, and the cat's eyes opened.

They were dull, filmed over. But they held him the way they had held him on the windowsill at the start of everything, when it had first seen that he saw. Arthur stopped breathing, the way you stop breathing for a thing you are afraid of startling.

And then, in the same breath, he took in the whole of what the road had cost since the white light: the fur gone matte along the spine, the small ribs counting under his fingers, the edges of the ears carrying that faint ghost of translucency that had been creeping inward since the underlevels. The weight of it had been falling out of the world by degrees, and he had been carrying the difference without filing it, the way you carry a debt you are not yet ready to count.

*I did not come to leave.*

The link faltered between the words, and he waited, kneeling in the warm dark, his heart in his throat.

*I came back to finish.*

The archivist in him reached for a drawer to file the sentence in, and found the drawer was not big enough — because every step of the road behind them stood in it: the windowsill, the deal, the crawl, the collar, the white light that had taken its body. Twenty years of a small black shape moving across the city's blind eye, always in one direction. He had read it as flight. It had been a return, measured and unhurried, all along — not running from anything, coming back to the one instrument built to hold it, carrying the one thing the instrument had always lacked.

It had needed a mind that remembered, a signature that would pass the doors, a body the city's eyes would not hold. The city had spent twenty years forging exactly that, paying it a clerk's wage, stamping it IRREPARABLE. He had been carrying it toward this room his whole life and had never known the direction. Even the lock on his spine was part of the instrument — a cage that had kept the remembering from being wasted, the way an earlier cage had kept the first singers from wasting their song. That thought he did not open.

*The eight sang,* it said, *and the singing was the taking. The city grew the ground of its thinking from what was left of them. They are the floor.*

He looked down at his hand, at the warm give under his palm, and for the first time he read the chamber entire — not as a room but as a document, and the document was the ground. The residue along the inner walls of the eight pods. The fine sediment of something grown and gone. Not dust, not waste — unspent song, and the singers with it, turned under.

A field ploughed once and sown with the dead, and out of that soil the city's whole mind had grown. The hub that cut memory out of forty million heads ran on the ground of the eight who had tried to bring life back, and under that same ground, sealed in the same warmth, lay the archive of every life the city had killed — the seed, the song, the ground, all one thing. One floor. The place that controls memory is the place that sequesters life. Not a sentence. Architecture, visible at last.

The pods kept the shape of what they had grown, the way a bowl keeps the shape of what it held, and what they had held was now what he knelt on.

'So the vault is what they were,' Arthur said aloud, to the room, to himself.

Vance heard only half the conversation in the room, and he said nothing to either half. He looked at the floor where Arthur's hand lay, then at Arthur, and Arthur watched him decide not to ask. The cat's voice had never once reached the captain's mind — the firewall was a wall in both directions — and so the transaction before him was a man talking to a small dim weight, and the air answering. He had carried his paper down the deepest stairs in the city to witness whatever a man would do at the bottom, and now, it seemed, he meant to witness it.

Arthur pressed his palm down until the give of the floor told him it was skin, not stone, and let the long breath of it run up his arm. Twenty years, and the ground had been breathing the city's thinking while the city cut thinking out of its people. He had sorted other people's forgetting for a living. This was where the forgetting came from, and it was warm.

*Not was,* the cat said. The words came one tier thinner, the way water thins going shallow. *Is. Still. Their song is in the ground. It never stopped being theirs. It has only been waiting for a voice strong enough to carry it.*

The three of them rested inside that sentence for a moment — the kneeling man, the standing man, and the floor under the whole city — and then the cat went on, in the register it had left:

*I watched the eight, before. I watched them sing until the singing was the taking, because no one had taught them the shape of it. I learned the lesson they died teaching: a key is only a key while it is unspent. So I kept it. All the years of your city, I kept it.*

It had been waiting to spend it here. Not on survival, not on escape — the vault had never needed the ninth to arrive; it had only ever needed the ninth to arrive with the key still whole, and with someone to hold the other end of the instrument.

The ninth pod stood at the centre of the ring, hatch swung back, its throat full of green — the transmitter, built to hold the ninth, and the ninth was it, and it in turn had been waiting for the rest of the instrument: the part the vault could never grow for itself. Not a singer. A mind. A living mind that remembered, for the sound to pass through, the way a voice needs a throat. It laid out the last terms of the deal struck on a windowsill days ago, flat as a clerk listing what a man owns:

*The sound has to move through a memory to resonate. One at a time. Yours. Each one goes in, the ground takes it, and the city hears it. And none of it comes back — not one memory, however you loved it. This is the price I have been quoting all along.* A pause. *You should know the whole of it before you answer.*

The mother, in the coat like a borrowed thing, narrow-shouldered — and on her face a grief Vance had only ever seen the empty version of, the shape of a room she had once stood in, filling at last, sound and all. She did not pull the boy back. She looked at Vance once, at the direction he had come from, and then she looked down at her son, and said, very low, almost to herself:

'Say it again.'

'Furry,' the boy said, and held the scrap up higher, an offering, and nothing amended it.

Vance took the pen from his coat. He opened the volume in the crook of his arm to the last page, to the certainty and the absence of it, and he lowered the pen to the paper — the first line he had written in the book since the one beneath the certainty — and he wrote, not an entry, no number:

> Today a child said the word. Nothing slid.

He closed the volume. The weight of it was the weight it had been when he came down: the page, the made hand, the two lines of it, now a third, and none of it for the grid. None of it had ever been for the grid.

The boy had not lowered the scrap. He had been waiting, holding the offering open the way a child holds a door. Vance looked at him, and for a long moment the captain and the child stood at the two ends of the old gesture. Vance said nothing — there was no word in him that could answer a child holding up fur in a city that had just remembered what fur was — so he gave the only thing he had that matched the shape of it: he looked back, once, toward the direction he had come from, where the levels kept the deep and the deep kept the two of them — the kneeling man, the small dark weight, the word standing in the air — and his hand came up.

One. The word was a child's.
Two. My hand knows the shape.
Three. There is no one above me to receive it.

He held the salute. The angle was the old one, drilled into him at nineteen, the crisp flat thing he had rendered ten thousand times to a system that had just amended itself out of existence — and he was not rendering it to that system. There was no system. There was a bearing, and the bearing was down, and what lived at the bottom of it had never once given him an order. It had never needed to.

The hand stayed up. The crowd went on remembering around it; the child's word stood in the space the uniform had made. Vance held the salute the way you hold a door for someone who has already gone.

Not for order.

# Chapter 17: Morning

He woke because something was missing.

The light had already reached the bottom of the deep — the first light of the world, come down the long bore of the ramp past three doors standing open, the color of no decision the city had ever made. He lay still and read the room by feel, item by item, the way he had read every room of his life, and one item answered wrong. No. The item was absent.

The hiss was not there.

It had stood at the back of his skull for twenty years — thirty centimeters of locked code and a dead uplink, a radio left between stations — and now there were no stations. There was only the quiet where the hiss had been, the way there is a quiet where a thing has been.

He had not known the hiss and the lock were the same thing, but the silence had the shape of the thing that had made it, and the shape was the shape of the radio, and the radio was off. Not between stations. Off. And with the hiss, the green had gone out of the room; the chamber stood in the first light, and nothing in it was enforcing anything.

His hands went flat to the floor in the old transfer, and there was no chair, and the procedure's object was gone. He stopped, and the stopping was a thing he had never had to do. He lay in the light with his palms on the warm ground, and the transfer waited for him like a sentence whose verb had been removed.

He had been kneeling when the night took him. At some point the kneeling had become leaning, and the leaning had become the way he woke now: propped against the base of the ninth pod, head bowed, the small dark weight on his chest. He could feel it breathing — slow, unhurried, the warmth of it arriving in his sternum — and he did not move for a while, because the morning was new and the weight was not. It was, he understood without finding a name for the understanding, the first morning of his life that held nothing to be afraid of.

The light lay level across the warm ground. The black thing stirred in it, the way it had always stirred — spine first, then shoulders, then the head coming up last — and it got down off him, one careful forepaw at a time, and settled on the warm floor beside him, looking at him.

'Stay,' he said.

The word came out of him whole, from a place he did not keep open, and he could not have said whether he had chosen it or whether it had been waiting for the morning. The black thing stopped. The head turned, and the old look held him — the look that had never once slid off him — and then it completed the descent and sat, tail laid around its feet, on the warm ground, staying.

He began the morning inventory.

It ran without him, the habit of twenty years: the day, the year, who he was. The shelves stood ready — clean, ordered, waiting — and most of them were empty. He walked the length of them the way you walk a corridor at closing, and the closing was the habit now; the inventory had become a shape, and the shape was nearly all that was left of it.

The box at the back — the room he had spent twenty years not opening — stood open. It had been carried out during the night, or the night before, and there was nothing in it to reach for; he closed it anyway, carefully, the way you close a door on a room you have loved and carried everything out of, and the closing was the last service of the discipline, and he performed it.

There was a note in the morning he did not open — it had no date, only temperature — and he did not open it now; it stayed, the one thing in the morning he did not reach for.

Then he counted.

One. Counting was what he did. It was the one whole thing left of a voice, and he did not remember whose; the voice had the shape of a room he had once stood in, and the room was gone, and the count remained, the way a shape remains when the room goes out of it. Two. Three.

He counted slowly, whole, the way you count a child somewhere else, and at ten his voice stood in the empty chamber, the first voice in the deep that morning — and something reported.

The right big toe.

It was the first thing in twenty years to report from below the waist, and it reported in order. The first thing was temperature: the warm give of the ground, not the twenty-one degrees of every morning of his life, arriving in the toe like a letter that had finally come through. The second was texture: the weave of the sock against the nail, and under it the hard, living give of the floor — the body's claim on the floor that the old arithmetic had compressed into the size of a coin, and this morning the floor stood for it whole.

The third was weight: the toe's own small gravity, the arch beyond it, the heel, the long claim of him, the whole forgotten architecture of standing, and this time it was not compressed. This time it stood at full size, and it did not close. The dead did not settle back over the toe like water closing over a stone. The water stayed back, and the toe stayed.

The news went up the wire that had been cut, and the cut had grown back across itself. The ball of the foot, taking the load, holding it. The arch, rising like a slow tide. The ankle, asked for the first time in twenty years, answering. The calf — muscles that had never been taught — stood to the work as if they had been waiting all their lives to be asked. The knee. The thigh, a column of him. The hip, and the whole stack of him above it, and the stack held.

He did not trust it. He turned and put both palms flat on the console under the pod's open throat, and he pushed — pushed against the console the way you test a floor by leaning, to see whether the thing under you will hold — and the floor held, and the legs held, and the console held, and he let the rest of his weight come home to the column of himself, all the way down to the two feet on the warm ground.

He was standing.

The room leaned. Not the room — the balance of him, a new instrument with no instruction book, and the lean went out through his arms into the console and came back a little less, and the tremor ran up out of his hips, long and deep, the tremor of his arms transferred at last to the legs' turn, and he held the console and did not let go, and the room steadied, and the steadiness was his own.

He had been holding his breath for the whole rise. He let it go, and the letting go did not take the stance with it. His hands had carried him for twenty years. They came off the console one finger at a time, and the legs held; he swayed once, a half-inch give at the knees that caught itself, and then he stood, and the only support in the world under him was the two feet under him, and they were his.

The room rearranged itself at standing height.

Every room of his remembering had arrived at the height of his knees and stayed there; this was the first room of his remembering that stood at its own height. The ring lay below him, a wide circle of warm ground. The eight pods around it were low, their open hatches at the height of his hip. The ninth stood against him, the transmitter, its throat open and dark at the height of his chest, the green gone out of it.

The bore of the ramp ran away past three open doors, and the morning stood in it. The place where a man had stood was empty; the doorway held only the light.

The light came level at this height, crossing the room the way light crosses a room it has decided to occupy, and he stood in it, and he was taller than everything in the room except the pod, the light, and the height of the world itself, which he had not known was a thing the world had. His hands hung at his sides, at the height of the console, unemployed, for the first time in their working lives.

The black thing watched him from the warm floor, head raised, the unspent look held level at the standing height of him. He watched it watch, and then it moved.

It rose from the floor in one motion — spine, shoulders, head, unhurried — and landed on him.

It landed on his lap, on the standing knees, and his knees felt it. The small, settled, impossible weight, warm and whole — every gram of it, felt, where the same weight had once settled on dead knees and he had felt nothing. In the first room it had landed on the dead length of him; it landed now on the living, and the only difference was everything. The legs took the addition without a step of give, and he stood under it, holding the weight of the thing he had carried across a city, at the height of the world.

He looked down.

A black, warm, steadily vibrating thing, on his lap, looking back up at him. He reached for the word — the whole reflex of his working life, the reach, the drawer — and the drawer opened into the place where a word had been. The form was there. The field was empty. And the thing on his knees fit the shape exactly, the way a thing fits the place it has been kept in, and he could not have said what it was, only that the fit was familiar, the way the shape of a room is familiar after the furniture goes out of it.

The vibration arrived in his jaw first, then his sternum, then the long bones — small, even, warm — and nothing came with it. No door opened. No register turned. No part of the world leaned in to listen. The key was spent; what remained was a low, even sound, kept up for no price and no reason, and nothing opened, and nothing needed to.

He stood at the centre of the ring under the morning, holding it, and he did not know what it was. He had had a word for it once — he kept the knowledge the way you keep a receipt — and he had had an afternoon, and a voice, and the count was all that was left of the voice, and the word was gone with the rest of it, into the open room.

He could not have said what he had lost. He knew only that he had lost something, because the shelves were empty and the shape remained — and the weight on his knees was familiar.

He had carried this weight before. He could not have said where, or when, or in which of the empty rooms. The weight was the shape, and the shape fitted the empty place, and the empty place was a room he had once stood in, and the room was gone, and the weight had returned to it.

His hand rose.

He watched it rise, the way a man watches a hand that has begun to do something of its own — up off his hip, across the air of the morning, toward the small dark weight on his knees — and it stopped, at the height of the empty place in him, and hung there, as if executing an instruction whose source was gone.

# Chapter 18: What the Body Keeps

The hand was still hanging.

He had stood through the whole of the morning in the one position, and the legs had held the whole way — the first long proof of them, taken without being asked. The light lay level across the ring, the first light of the world, come down the bore of the ramp past the three open doors. It crossed the warm ground the way light crosses a room it has decided to occupy. The small dark weight sat on the living knees, patient as a debt someone had decided not to collect. The hand hung above it at the height of the empty place in him, executing an instruction whose source was gone.

He watched the hand. It was a new economy, watching a thing all the way through; the morning held no register, no schedule, no next thing, and the whole taxonomy of his life had been unstaffed in the night. The room stood at its own height around him, nothing in it enforcing anything, and there was no sound anywhere in the world that he could hear except the two of them keeping time, and he stood in the light and watched, and the hand hung.

The legs trembled once, a half-inch give at the knees that caught itself, and he waited for the old arithmetic to close over them, and it did not. The water stayed back. He had been standing for a long time, and the standing had begun to feel less like a proof than like a place, and the hand had hung the whole while, and it was the only unfinished thing in the morning.

Then it fell.

It fell the way a thing falls when it is finished being suspended — not dropped, not aimed, completed. Down across the air and the light, and the black thing did not flinch, did not arrange itself, did not perform the receiving; it let the hand arrive. The fingers came to rest in the warm hollow behind the jaw and found the beginning of a motion there.

The hand knew the motion. He did not know it, and then he did, the way you recognize a place you have only ever reached at night. The motion was old, older than the empty place in him, older than the morning; it was slow and even, and it had a time to it, and the time was the time of his counting — one, and the hand drew its long stroke; two, and the stroke returned — the count that had been left of a voice, kept for twenty years in the chest, spent now in the fingers. The hand counted, and the black thing leaned into the stroke, a small whole lean, the weight settling into it the way the stroke was settling into it, and the vibration arrived, low and even, under the palm and down the long bones of him.

The sound was real, and warm, and steady.

He had known its other sounds, down all the long stair of the remembering — the silent yawn, the performed stretch, the folded eyes with the verdict in them, the whole soundless rhetoric of a thing that had never once been taken by surprise. None of that was in this sound. It had no arrangement in it. It did not rise for effect or hold for a price; it simply continued, the way a thing continues when it has decided to continue, and it was the first sound of its life, he understood without knowing how he understood it, that was being kept for nothing.

It entered him the way warmth enters a room when a register is finally open: through the palm first, then the wrist, up the long bone of the arm to the shoulder, where it pooled. He had known warmth his whole life, calibrated and uniform, twenty-one degrees, and gratitude for it had been work, and he had done the work daily. This warmth was not calibrated. It was uneven by the half-second, alive the way weather is alive, and nothing anywhere was calculating it, and it was the first warmth of his life that had not arrived with a bill.

The stroke went on at the count's time. Under his palm the fur was warm, and the spine curved to the stroke the way a road gives to a foot, and the tail lay loose along his wrist, writing nothing. He remembered the tail writing. In the first room, at the window, it had written long lazy verdicts and laid them down, and the eyes had folded shut like a ledger closing, and every motion had carried its own announcement, none of it for him, all of it for him. This motion carried no announcement. It had no one watching it but him, and he was not an audience; he was the other end of the stroke.

His hands. The thought arrived whole, the way the morning had: his hands had pushed him for twenty years, had sorted and sentenced and buried what they saw, had held the console through the rising, had carried the small dark weight down the stair of the dead city through the three doors, and now they kept it. Of all the work they had ever been given, this was the first that was not work. He watched them in the first light, the way you watch a machine you have trusted, doing at last the thing it was made for, and he did not know what the thing was called, and the hands did not care. Wherever he reached, the drawer opened into the empty field, and the hands went on, and it was enough that they went on.

At the top of a stroke his hand lifted — the old reach, the drawer, the whole reflex of his working life — and the drawer opened into the place where the word had been, and the field was empty, and he stood with his hand above the small dark weight at the height of the empty place, and the hand hung there again, waiting for the source of the instruction, which was gone.

The black thing pressed its head up into the open palm.

That was the whole of it — a pressure, deliberate, unhurried, the old unhurriedness with all the advertisement gone out of it. The head came up and the palm came down and closed over the warm skull, and the motion resumed, and the sound with it, and he understood, in the way the body understands before the mind is consulted, that this was what the animal had finally learned: to take what it was given.

It had watched him give. It had watched him give the afternoon, give the voice, give the word, hand over hand, down the long burning, and it had never understood it — the one thing in all its long and patient existence it had never been able to price — and at the end it had stopped pricing. Giving was not a transaction; it was the whole motion, the stroke and the sound and the staying, all one motion, given and kept at once. The weight on his knees was not a debt anymore. The sound was not a key anymore. The key had been spent in the night; what remained was a sound that nothing was being taken for.

He remembered the saying. Somewhere in the deep, with the animal's throat against his and the sound going up through him, he had said the word once, aloud, and it had been the most expensive thing he had ever done, and it had been worth it. He did not remember the word. He remembered the surrender, the small give in his throat, the way a thing gives way — and the worth. The word had burned with the rest. The worth had not. It had gone down into the body and stayed there, the way the count had stayed, the way this stroke stayed now, under the palm, at the count's time.

He said the one word he had left.

'Stay.'

It stayed. The head rested in the palm, and the weight settled deeper into the living lap — the lap that had once felt nothing, the weight that had once been impossible, arrived at last at full size, felt gram by gram — and the black thing turned once, deliberately, and settled the tail around its feet the way it had settled once at a window, in the first room, when the settling had been a performance. This settling was just the settling. Nobody was watching it but him, and it stayed, and the light came level across them both.

The legs had held him through the whole morning, and standing was no longer a proof; it was just standing. He lowered himself, one hand still stroking, the other braced on the warm ground, the weight riding down with him without a step of give, and sat with his back against the pod, and the light came level at sitting height, and the sky stood at the end of the bore, a color no city had decided on, and there was no schedule anywhere in it.

Nothing in the morning needed answering. For twenty years every morning had been a tally of what was owed — the gratitude, the doors that opened for the badge, the schedule that ran like a wire through him — and the tally had gone out in the night with the rest of it. He sat in the light, and the light required nothing back, and he gave himself to it the way you give yourself to a floor you have finally stopped distrusting.

The note stayed where it had been left, unopened — no date, only temperature — the one thing in the morning he did not reach for. He reached for other things one last time, the way you reach for a thing you have loved and carried everything out of, and the drawer opened, and the form was there, and the field was empty, and it was all right. The empty place was the oldest thing he owned. The body had kept everything the mind had been made to spend — the count in the voice, the standing in the legs, the stroke in the hands, in a place no burn had ever reached and none ever would.

He had forgotten the word. His hands had not.
