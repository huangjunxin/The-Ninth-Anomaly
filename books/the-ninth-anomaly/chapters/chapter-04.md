# Chapter 4: Paper and Dust

The report was forty-one lines long. He read it twice. Twice was the habit — older than the rank — and it rested on a rule learned before twenty: words did not stay where they were put, and a man who read a thing once was reading what was left of it.

Renn: drew at 01:49. Does not know why.
Dalby: drew at 01:49. Does not know why.

Two lines of testimony, nearly identical, from two men who had never done anything in the same way. He knew them. Renn was forty, carried the job like a borrowed coat; Dalby was young and had stood, until last night, on his toes, the way a man stands when he expects the floor to move. Different men. Same gap. The hole in each of them had the same dimensions, as if the same coin had been lifted from both pockets in the same minute.

One. Two witnesses.
Two. One hole.
Three. The hole had edges.

That was the whole of it, and it was everything. A forgotten thing left no edges. He had spent a career in rooms where the cut had been made — the wire between concept and feeling slipped out, the mind slid off the shape, and the room came out smooth, finished, dustless, as if nothing had ever been there. This was not that. This was a thing taken out of time: a cause, a why, lifted out of two trained men, and the place it had stood was still raw.

Four. The record closed itself.

*No anomaly present. Unit stood down. No record.* In a city that never stopped recording. The grid wrote states, not sentences; the close-out read like a clerk's hand, and no clerk had been in that flat.

He called them in. They stood on the worn strip of floor before his desk, and he asked the same question twice, because that was the habit too.

'Why did you draw?'

'I don't know, sir.' Dalby's toes were flat now. He stood like a man in a room he no longer trusted.

'Renn.'

'The instruments painted a shape on the sill, sir.' Renn answered the way he did everything, patiently, in a coat that no longer quite fitted. 'That's the last thing I've got. There was a shape on the sill, and then — ' The sentence had no referent. It died in the flat; it died again at the desk.

'The shape of what?'

The two men said nothing. They had been inside that flat for ten minutes and lost the same three minutes; they remembered the apologies, the apology going back and forth like a coin, the door — Renn was sure of the sound the splintered frame made when he wedged the panel, sure of it — and neither of them could say why any of it had begun.

'Report's closed, sir,' Renn said, with the careful courtesy of a man offering a fact he did not quite believe. 'Says no record.'

'Yes,' Vance said. 'It does.'

He dismissed them and watched them go: the borrowed coat, the flat-footed tread, two men walking out of his office with the same hole in each of them. The grid had a word for what it did to citizens. It had no word for what had been done to officers.

The log lived in the bottom drawer of his desk, under a lock that had never been wired to anything. It was a bound book, paper, the third of his tenure, and it was the only unamendable thing in the building.

That was the whole argument, made at twenty, never revised: the grid could amend anything in its own medium — a file, a close-out, a history, this building's history, amended in the night, without residue. And a mind, any mind, his own, took the cut the way any head took it; he had stood in rooms where it was made and watched men walk out smooth and finished; he had written the reports the grid then rewrote in its own quiet hand.

Paper could not be amended. Ink dried. Pages tore. A hand that went back to a page left the pressure of its returning in the fiber, and pressure was a witness. Firewalls could not edit paper.

The book had weight, and he trusted weight. The smell of its paper came up when he opened it, the smell of a thing that had to be opened to be changed. He took up the pen and wrote:

> 3-0711. Time 07:20. Two men. One gap. Same dimensions.
> The record closed itself: *no record*, in a city that never stops recording.
> The close-out is not in the grid's hand. Question: whose?
> Watch the flat.

The resident file came up the way files came up: a life reduced to a chute. Arthur M., 28. Retainer, marked, locked. *IRREPARABLE.* Clerk, Forbidden Archive. An address — the address from the report. The surplus. The sill.

He read the name twice.

Arthur.

The name did not arrive as a file. It arrived as a temperature, the way some things did: heat through a window that should not have been open, thirty-one degrees, a summer the city no longer admitted it had had. A household, flagged. A case number carrying the old year, 1998, before the grid changed its numbering. A child's picture book, confiscated, graded high — the grade his. He had led the detail himself. That was the assignment he remembered best, and the one he remembered least, and both were true.

He remembered the heat. He remembered the book in his gloved hand, the weight of it, light — the way a confiscated thing was always heavier than it had been in its owner's keeping. And he remembered the child: not the center but the edge of the raid, small, behind a door that had stayed closed, because there was a voice. The boy's voice, going on through the panels, reciting something, and his men standing with their backs to it, the way men stand with their backs to what they have no orders about.

The old volume was on the shelf behind his desk — the first volume, the one whose spine had gone soft. The night was in it, written in the hand he had had at twenty, in the summer of 1998. He put his hand on the spine, held it, and took it back. He had never read that page all the way through. He did not intend to begin tonight.

He issued the envelope himself: the flat, watched. The retainer, held in place. No contact. And he did not commit to the terminal what he thought, because what he thought had no grid category — that the city had sent two men into that flat and taken nothing out of it, and the men had come back out lighter by a cause they could not name, and the flat's file carried a stamp he had seen on exactly one other file in his life, and that one had been a child's, twenty years, unrevised.

He stayed. The report would not close, and neither would he. Near midnight he wrote:

> 3-0712. Item: the retainer of the flat.
> I knew the name before I opened the file.
> Twenty years, and the book is still on the sill.

* * *

The door was still broken. That was the first thing Arthur filed, at 02:40, with the cat's silence filling the room the way it had filled it all evening: the panel stood wedged against the frame exactly as the older officer had left it, a courtesy with no repair behind it, and the city had no record of the door at all. A door it had not logged was not a door it knew about. He had turned that sentence over a dozen times since the morning; now he was going to leave through it.

He packed by the discipline, which was to say he packed almost nothing. The chair's pouch took the ration pack — the last he would draw under his own name. It took the badge that had opened every door of his adult life. It took the blanket off his knees, folded small, the way he folded everything, small and exact, leaving nothing behind. The blanket had lain across the dead length of him for years, and it was the only thing that had ever lain there without asking anything back — and the cat would ride on it.

The cat watched him from the middle of the desk, where it had watched all evening without comment — matte, dusty, the black of a dye at the end of its wash, a shade thinner than it had been that morning, the price of the second it had sold him. It had slept in long flat drifts, wrong, like a thing unplugged, and the waking was work: the spine, then the shoulders, then the head coming up last, as if something in it had to be wound. It made no sound. When he came back from the door it was sitting up, and the sitting had cost it, and it looked at the folded blanket, then at the door, and its eyes folded shut once — not a blink, a seal passing over a document — the smile of a broker watching a client reach the number.

'Don't,' he said to it, quietly. The word came out before he could file it; he had built a grammar out of that word, and it had never once been addressed to a thing that was listening. 'Don't move. Don't sound. Don't spend anything.'

The cat, on the blanket on his dead knees, under the unlogged door, yawned.

A slow, deliberate hinge of a mouth, silent, in the dark of the broken frame — the yawn of a thing that had yawned once into the muzzles of two weapons — and the silence was not an absence but an announcement.

He counted to ten, the old way — *when I count to ten, you'll be somewhere else* — and, as it had always happened, the door opened first. Only this time the hand was his own. He pushed the panel aside and rolled through the first door of his life that he had opened himself, and he was not yet on the far side of the ten.

The corridor was a set of angles and times, and he read them the way he read everything: the camera came round, held, passed on; he moved in the blind four seconds at the end of its sweep, the chair's wheels finding the seams of the floor the way his hands found the seams of a file. The elevator logged his weight. He let it. A citizen may leave his home at any hour; at 02:52 his account was still a citizen's.

The cordon lay along the boundary road, north of the block. He knew it the way he knew every procedure the city had ever run — thoroughly, and without remembering them. The old rotation tables sat in the same condemned batch as the engineer's file: dead infrastructure, formats and schedules the city had stopped using and never stopped obeying. The grid, when it took over the boundary, had kept the skeleton of the paper rotation underneath, the way a new hand keeps the old script's habit. Nobody remembered why the seams were where they were. The archive remembered. Arthur remembered.

The pairs turned at midnight and every three hours after. The seam at 03:00 was the long one: the outgoing pair walked the north arc back to the muster yard to log off, because the log-off was a ritual nobody had shortened, and the incoming pair mustered in the yard, and for fifteen minutes — 03:00 to 03:15 — the north arc had no boot on it. And once a night, at 03:09, the grid re-synced its eyes: the north camera lost the feed for six seconds. He had filed both facts years ago, under *Procedures, dead, still running,* never expecting to use them. He used them now, because the checkpoints sat on the lanes and the threads, and the boundary's only true gap was the one the city had forgotten it kept.

He reached the ramp's mouth at 02:57. The north arc's boots came past above him — two men, unhurried, the sound of men with all night and the right to use it — and passed, and the noise receded toward the yard. He waited in the shadow of the abutment with the cat's warmth on the blanket and the box shut under the discipline, and counted, because counting was what he did.

03:00. The arc went empty.
03:09. The camera's eye, high on its pole, went dark.

He was already moving. The gate under the abutment was older than the cordon, older than the grid's memory of itself; its key was catalogued in the same dead grammar as a hundred other keys, and he fed it the digits one-handed while the other held the wheel, and the gate opened without sound. He pushed — the way he had pushed for twenty years, arms strong enough to carry the rest of him — and the ramp fell away down and across the boundary line, the wheels making their soft sound on the old tarmac; the far abutment came up and over him, and he stopped.

He had been under the camera's eye for six seconds. The eye came back. Nothing on the feed. The seam closed over him the way water closes over a coin.

He sat with his shoulders heaving, the cat still and warm and soundless on the blanket. It had not moved. It had not made a sound. It was spending nothing, which was the only gift it had to give, and he understood that he was being carried through his own escape by the only creature in the city that had ever carried him — on his own dead knees.

Then the world's attention changed.

He felt it the way he had felt the building change last night, when it ran its reconciliation and found a surplus: a room's temperature shifting, unsourced. Far along the line, past the ramp's lip, the checkpoint crew had stopped moving — all of them, at the same moment, the way men stop when a number comes up. He could not see his own signature, but he had lived twenty-eight years under the city's attention and understood its grammar, and this was the sentence the night had been building since 01:00, last night:

His signature had crossed a line it had no permission to cross. The envelope was breached. The movement was anomalous. The word had existed all along; the city had always kept it; it had simply never once been his.

*Fugitive.*

He filed it precisely, the way he filed everything: the category, the date, the minute, the fact that he had filed himself. He was a fugitive now, on his own signature, under his own name, and the whole city — which had never once seen him — was going to start looking.

The hiss in his skull did not change pitch. It had never changed. He had not expected it to.

He looked back once. The flat was somewhere behind the boundary — the broken door, the wedged panel, the room at twenty-one degrees the city no longer knew existed. He had lived his whole life inside the city's attention. He had stepped out of it. It had noticed, and the notice was the price of the step — a price he had known before he took it.

The cat's eyes folded shut once, slowly — a seal passing over a document. And it said, without sound, the words arriving the way files arrive, already written, already his:

*"Welcome to the surplus column. It's larger than the city admits."*

He pushed the chair into the dark, toward the levels the current maps did not keep. The dust of the ramp settled on his wheels. The box stayed shut. Behind him, at the four corners of the boundary, the grid wrote his new classification in its own hand, smoothly, without residue. He did not see it. He was somewhere else — for the first time in his life, his own doing.

* * *

The alert arrived at 03:14. Vance read it twice.

*Signature: ARTHUR M. Movement: anomalous. Envelope: breached. Time 03:09. Point of exit: north seam. Status: fugitive.*

The envelope he had issued himself, twelve hours ago. The retainer he had ordered held in place. And the boy — the boy had gone out through the one seam no current map showed, because the seam existed only in the archived rotation, in dead procedures nobody remembered.

One. The seam is not in the current maps.
Two. The seam is in the archive.
Three. The boy is the archive.
Four. He knew the dark between the posts.

Vance switched the screen off and looked out at the boundary lights going about their work. The city was forgetting something, and it did not know it was forgetting, and the one man who could name it had walked out through the dark between the posts, as if the map had always been his.

He took up the log. He turned back to the midnight page. He wrote beneath the entry, without revision:

> He knows what we are forgetting.
